## Diabetes mellitus — definition and scale (slide 4)

**Definition:** a group of **metabolic diseases characterized by hyperglycemia.**

**Three causes:**
- Decreased insulin secretion
- Impaired insulin action (**insulin resistance**)
- A combination of both

**Epidemiology**
- Estimated **37.3 million** adults in the US; incidence rising
- ⊙ She noted the textbook figure lags publication — current CDC data is higher
- Ethnic and racial minority populations affected disproportionately

★ **Why the disparity — her answer, asked of the class:** nutrition available in the environment; access to care; time and money to reach care even when it exists (multiple jobs, copays); health literacy oriented toward treating illness rather than preventing it; and cultural food staples.

**What uncontrolled diabetes leads the nation in** ★
- Leading cause of **non-traumatic amputations** (non-traumatic = not from an accident)
- Leading cause of **end-stage renal disease**
- Leading cause of **adult-onset blindness**
- **7th leading cause of death** in the US

★ Her framing: by the time a patient is first diagnosed, the damage has often already been done — it simply had not produced a symptom yet. Once you reach gangrene requiring amputation, or ESRD, that damage is **irreversible**; all treatment can do from there is prevent it from worsening. This is the entire argument for annual wellness visits and preventive labs.

**Word roots** ★ — hyper = high, glyc = sugar, -emia = in the blood; poly = many; -uria = urine; -dipsia = thirst; -phagia = eating; lipo = fat; -lysis = breakdown; -genesis = making; euglycemia = normal blood sugar.

## The pancreas (slide 6)

**Location:** behind the stomach. Has **both endocrine and exocrine** function.

| Endocrine — islets of Langerhans | Exocrine — enzymes |
|---|---|
| **Beta cells → insulin** | **Amylase** |
| **Alpha cells → glucagon** | **Lipase** |
| **Delta cells → somatostatin** | |

★ **Clinical hook:** amylase and lipase are the enzymes checked when pancreatitis (inflammation of the pancreas) is suspected.

★ **Her framing point:** insulin is a **natural hormone** first. Most people hear "insulin" and think medication. It only becomes a medication because the body's own hormone is not doing its job, so an **exogenous** form is given.

## Insulin (slides 7–8) ★★

**Secreted by the beta cells.** It is a **storage hormone** — stored in the beta cells and released when needed.

| Insulin release is STIMULATED by | Insulin release is INHIBITED by |
|---|---|
| **Parasympathetic** stimulation | **Sympathetic** stimulation |
| Elevated serum glucose | Low serum glucose |
| Increased GI hormones | High levels of insulin |
| **Increased serum potassium** | |

**Why parasympathetic stimulates:** rest-and-digest is the state in which you absorb a meal. Fight-or-flight (sympathetic) needs glucose **up**, so it shuts insulin off and turns glucagon on.

### Insulin action — memorize by target

| Target | Action |
|---|---|
| **Glucose** | Metabolizes glucose for energy; **increases glycogenesis** (transport of glucose into cells → increased glycogen synthesis) → **lowers blood sugar** |
| **Fats** | **Increases lipogenesis** (glucose into adipose cells for storage); **inhibits lipolysis** (breakdown of fat) |
| **Liver** | **Inhibits glycogenolysis** (conversion of stored glycogen back to glucose) |
| **Protein** | Facilitates transport of **amino acids** into cells |
| **Potassium** | Facilitates **intracellular transport of potassium** |

★ **Why the potassium line matters:** insulin drives potassium into cells. That is why insulin (with dextrose) is a treatment for hyperkalemia, and why treating DKA with insulin drops serum potassium.

★ **Why she insisted on learning insulin as a hormone first:** you cannot understand the mechanism of the oral agents without it. Metformin, for example, works on the **liver** — you only understand that if you know what insulin does to the liver.

### Glucagon (slide 9)

- Acts to keep blood glucose **elevated**; the **antagonist** to insulin (antagonist = works against)
- **Stimulated by:** sympathetic stimulation, **decreased** serum glucose, secretion of growth hormone
- **Decreased stimulation by:** increased glucose levels
- **Functions:** stimulates **glycogenolysis**, stimulates **gluconeogenesis**, enhances **lipolysis**

★ Insulin and glucagon are never released together — the negative feedback loop runs one or the other, and both are aiming at **euglycemia**.

★ Exogenous glucagon/glucose forms for hypoglycemia she named: oral glucose gel, glucose tablets, IV D50, IM or subQ glucagon.

### Somatostatin (slide 10)

- **Interferes with the release of growth hormone** from the pituitary gland
- Exerts a **hypoglycemic** effect

⚠ **Correction.** In the transcript a student offered "growth hormone" as another name for somatostatin and it was accepted. Somatostatin is **not** growth hormone — it is **growth hormone-inhibiting hormone (GHIH)**. The slide itself is correct: it interferes with GH release. Answer the slide, not the transcript.

### Quick recap slide (slide 11) ★

- Our cells can use **only glucose** as energy, **not glycogen**
- **Insulin's goal:** decrease blood sugar and store energy for **LATER**
- **Glycogen's goal:** release stored sugar into blood for energy **NOW**

Sequence to be able to trace: carbohydrate intake → glucose → used for immediate energy, excess **stored in the liver as glycogen** → during fasting, sleep, a skipped meal, exercise, or an insulin overdose, glucagon triggers **glycogenolysis** → glycogen converts back to **glucose** → used as energy.

## Type 1 diabetes (slides 13–14)

**Characterized by destruction of the beta cells.**

**Causes:** genetic · immunologic · environmental
★ Autoimmune destruction is the usual mechanism. Environmental toxins and viral triggers are both recognized; often the trigger is never identified, and identifying it does not change the fact that an autoimmune condition, once present, is permanent. Having one autoimmune condition raises the risk of developing another.

**Results in:**
- Decreased insulin production
- Elevated blood glucose levels
- **Increased glucose production by the liver**

**Onset is usually abrupt** — days to weeks. Frequently the presentation *is* **DKA**.
**Approximately 5%** of patients with diabetes have type 1 (so ~95% are type 2).

★ **Age at diagnosis:** formerly called juvenile diabetes; that name is gone because **type 1 can be diagnosed at any age.** It is more common in childhood, especially with a genetic component, but her own new patient was **46** and presented in DKA.

**Treatment: insulin only. Never an oral antidiabetic agent — ever.** ★★

### Manifestations (slide 14)

Acute onset; symptoms depend on the level of hyperglycemia.

**Classic triad — "the 3 Ps"** ★★
1. **Polyuria** — excessive urination
2. **Polydipsia** — excessive thirst
3. **Polyphagia** — excessive hunger

**Other manifestations**
- **Glycosuria** (sugar in the urine)
- **Weight loss**
- **Fatigue**
- **Sudden vision changes**
- **Nausea, vomiting or abdominal pain if the patient is in DKA**

★ Symptom severity does not track the number reliably. She has seen a patient at 90 who is weak, fatigued and confused, a patient at 40 who is unresponsive and another at 40 who just "feels off," and patients at 250 and 450 with no symptoms at all. Treat the patient, not only the glucometer.

★ Why weight loss is missed: people around the patient every day do not notice; the patient does not notice until clothes loosen or they step on a scale. Ten to twenty pounds can go before it registers.

## Diagnostic criteria (slide 15) ★★ — memorize the four numbers

| Test | Diagnostic threshold |
|---|---|
| **Casual (random) blood glucose** | **> 200 mg/dL** |
| **Fasting blood glucose** | **> 126 mg/dL** |
| **Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)** | **> 200 mg/dL** |
| **Hemoglobin A1c** | **> 6.5%** |

*(Slide refers to Chart 46-2. The slide's own reference note is to the 15th edition; the chart number differs in the 16th.)*

★ **Why random >200 works as a threshold:** in a person without diabetes, the negative feedback loop keeps glucose under 200 **no matter what they just ate**. Insulin is also released in tiny increments throughout the day, not only in response to eating.

★ **Fasting** means nothing to eat or drink for **6–8 hours**.

★ **The OGTT is a stimulation test** — tie this straight back to Lecture 1. You drink a concentrated sugar load, which *should* stimulate the negative feedback loop to release insulin and hold glucose down. Failure to hold it down is the positive result. Most people meet it in pregnancy screening for gestational diabetes, but it is used for diabetes generally and for other conditions affecting glucose (sepsis, PCOS, metabolic disorders can all raise or lower glucose without diabetes).

★ **Hemoglobin A1c** measures roughly **3 months** of glycemic control, because it measures glucose bound to the red blood cell and reflects the red cell's lifespan. This is why A1c is used both to **diagnose** and to **monitor**: the patient who blames a high glucose on a 10-day vacation still has to account for the other two and a half months.

**A1c targets** ★
- **Diagnosis** of diabetes: **≥ 6.5%**
- **ADA treatment goal: < 7%**

⚠ A1c level does not dictate treatment by itself. She has patients at 8 and at 13; a 13 may mean nonadherence, or it may mean the wrong drug for that person. Some medications simply do not work well in some people, and genetic testing to predict that exists only for a few drug classes.

## Treatment of type 1 (slides 16–17)

**Five components:** medication management (**insulin only, no oral antidiabetic meds**) · nutrition · exercise · blood glucose monitoring · patient education

### Treatment goals (slide 17)
- Achieving blood glucose control — **euglycemia without hypoglycemia**
- **ADA recommends Hgb A1c < 7%**
- Prevention of secondary complications:
  - **Retinopathy** — damage to the vessels of the retina → vision loss
  - **Nephropathy** — damage to the vessels of the kidney → renal failure
  - **Neuropathy** — nerve damage

★ The "yo-yo" concept she returned to repeatedly: swinging between highs and lows is not control. Damage accrues during the highs even when the patient feels fine afterward. This is why simply covering a cupcake with extra insulin is not management.

⚠ Neuropathy is not exclusive to diabetes — she named B6 overdose and carpal tunnel as other causes, and some of those are reversible.

### Nutrition and exercise (type 1)
- Carbohydrate counting is essential because **insulin dosing is based on carb intake**
- Reading **nutrition labels**, especially **serving size** — the "low fat / reduced fat" label is per serving, and most people eat two or three servings
- Choosing foods by **glycemic index** to avoid spikes

**Exercise target:** aerobic exercise **30 minutes, at least 5 days a week = 150 minutes/week.**
★ **But set attainable goals.** Prescribing 150 minutes to a sedentary patient, or "lose 100 pounds," produces a patient who quits before starting. Start at 30 minutes a week, or movement during commercial breaks, and build. Break it into segments if they cannot sustain 30 minutes. If they fall off one week, that is not a reason to stop.

### Self-monitoring of blood glucose (slide 18) ★
- Recommended **2–4 times daily** for those on insulin therapy: **before meals** and at **bedtime**
- Recommended **less often** for those not receiving insulin (type 2 patients frequently do not check daily, and insurers often will not cover the supplies)
- Keep a **logbook** of glucose levels — and record symptoms alongside the numbers
- **Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)**

★★ **Never take insulin without knowing the blood sugar.** Her patient who filled the long-acting insulin, skipped the short-acting, and refused fingersticks is injecting blind — the two outcomes are a hypoglycemic coma or a return trip in DKA.

★ Frequency follows the regimen: long-acting once daily may mean checking once a day; insulin with meals means at least 3 times a day; sliding scale with meals and bedtime means 4 times a day.

★★ **CGM is not an insulin pump.** A CGM **monitors only** — receiver, sensor, transmitter, and it delivers nothing. An insulin pump **administers** insulin. Do not confuse them.
- CGM trend arrows: sideways = steady · straight up = rising fast · angled up = rising slowly · straight down = falling fast · angled down = falling slowly. The arrow tells you how fast you must intervene, and preset alarms sound for highs and lows.

## Insulin therapy

### Indications (slide 19) — FIVE
1. **Type 1 diabetes**
2. **Hyperglycemia that doesn't respond to oral agents**
3. **Allergy or other contraindication to oral agents**
4. **Pregnancy**
5. **Late in type 2 management**

⊙ She added: hospitalized type 2 patients are frequently placed on insulin temporarily after surgery, injury, illness or infection, because healing requires euglycemia. That does not mean they go home on it.

★ Why type 2 eventually needs insulin: it starts as insulin resistance with functioning beta cells, but over time — especially with poor control — the beta cells become damaged and release less and less.

### Insulin characteristics (slide 20)
Dose is **individual**, based on blood glucose level. **Self-monitoring is the crucial piece.** Categorized by **rate of absorption.**

| Type | Example | Onset | Peak | Duration | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rapid acting** | **Lispro (Humalog)** | **15–30 min** | **30–90 min** | **< 5 hours** | **2× as fast as regular.** Give **5–15 min before a meal**; patient **MUST eat within 15 min** |
| **Short acting (regular)** | **Humulin R**, marked "R" | **30–60 min** | | | **Clear** solution; **may be given IV**; give **15 min before meals**; may be given alone or with a longer-acting insulin |
| **Intermediate** | **NPH** | **1–1.5 hours** | **4–12 hours** | | **White, cloudy**; may be given **Q12H as a basal dose** |
| **Long acting (basal)** | **Glargine (Lantus)** | | **"Peakless"** | ~24 h | **Cannot be mixed with any other insulin**; once daily, any time of day, but **the same time every day**; absorbed very slowly |
| **Rapid-acting inhalation powder** | **Afrezza** | | | | Rarely seen |

### The rules that get tested ★★

1. **Regular insulin is the ONLY insulin that can be given IV.**
2. **Long-acting (glargine/basal) is NEVER mixed with another insulin in the same syringe.** It may be given at the same time as another insulin — in **two separate syringes**.
3. **Rapid or short acting MAY be mixed with intermediate (NPH).**
4. **Do not give rapid or short acting until the meal tray is physically present.** ★ Giving insulin at 11:30 for a tray that arrives at 12:15 puts the patient on the floor. See the food cart on the unit before you draw it up.
5. **Peak = when the insulin is most effective**, and therefore when hypoglycemia is most likely — but hypoglycemia can occur at any time, including from a wrong dose, so monitoring is never limited to the peak window.

**Appearance check** ★
- Regular/rapid = **clear**; should show **no particles or precipitate** when rolled
- NPH = **uniformly cloudy**; roll between the hands, and it should stay one uniform color
- **Discard** any insulin that is lumpy, frosted, clumped, or has a **whitish coating** inside the bottle

### Insulin delivery (slide 26)
**Vial and syringe · insulin pen (dial up units) · jet injector · insulin pump**

- Pens are easier and more accurate for patients but more expensive
- **Insulin pump:** carries a programmed prescription, but **it must be acted upon** — the patient enters their glucose and carb intake and confirms the dose. It does **not** deliver automatically.
- ★ A patient admitted with their own pump should have a **signed contract**, because the facility cannot take responsibility for what the pump delivers. **The nurse never presses the button, changes the dose, or enters carbs.** If the patient is confused and cannot operate it, the pump should not be used — the nurse administers instead. You still check and document the glucose and the amount given "per patient."

### Storage and preparation (slide 27)
- **Refrigerate when not in use**
- **Vials in use may be kept at room temperature for up to one month**
- **Avoid extreme temperatures — don't store it in the car**
- Cloudy insulins: **gently invert or roll between the hands** (never shake)
- **Inspect NPH for a whitish coating inside the bottle**
- **May mix rapid and intermediate**
- ★ Facility vials carry a label with either the **open date** or the **expiration date** — know which convention your facility uses. Glucometer control solutions expire from opening too.

### Injection technique (slide 28)
- **Rotate sites to prevent lipodystrophy** (loss/change of the fatty tissue from repeated use of one site); **rotate sites every 2–3 weeks**
- **Exercise or heat application accelerates absorption** — so do not massage or rub after injecting
- **90-degree angle** for average or overweight patients; **45 degrees if very thin**
- **No need to aspirate or massage** — subcutaneous tissue has no vessel to hit
- Dispose of needles and syringes per local regulations
- ⊙ Abdomen: stay at least **1 inch from the umbilicus**; check the MAR for the last site rather than relying on the patient's memory
- ★ Technique: hold it **like a dart**, not with a thumb on the plunger (you will start dispensing before the needle is in, and with 1–3 unit doses that error is the whole dose). Insert quickly — going slowly is what hurts. Hold for a count of three before withdrawing so the full dose is delivered.

## Complications of insulin therapy (slides 29–30) ★★

- **Local allergic reactions** — a localized rash; generally self-limiting
- **Systemic allergic reactions** — rare, but significant
- **Insulin lipodystrophy** — prevented by **regular rotation of sites**
- **Resistance to injected insulin** — can develop at a repeatedly used or calloused site
- **Morning hyperglycemia**, which has three separate causes:

| | **Insulin waning** | **Dawn phenomenon** | **Somogyi effect** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | The dose simply wears off before morning | **Elevated growth hormone in the AM hours: ↑GH → ↑glucose.** A natural diurnal event | The body's **response to hypoglycemia during sleeping hours** (roughly 2–3 AM) produces rebound **early morning hyperglycemia** |
| **2–3 AM glucose** | Normal or gradually rising | Normal, then rising toward morning | **LOW** |
| **Management** | Adjust the dose or the timing | **Change the time of NPH from dinner to bedtime** | **Decrease the evening NPH, or add a bedtime snack** |

★★ **The memory hook she gave: "Somogyi — so low."** The blood sugar goes *so low* in the middle of the night. That 2–3 AM glucose is the single test that separates Somogyi from dawn phenomenon, and it determines whether you add insulin or take it away — get it backwards and you make the patient worse.

⚠ **Mechanism correction.** In the transcript the rebound was described as the body "releasing too much insulin." It is the opposite: the body responds to nocturnal hypoglycemia by releasing **counterregulatory hormones** — glucagon, epinephrine, cortisol and growth hormone — which drive glucose **up**. The slide's wording ("the body's response to hypoglycemia during sleeping hours") is correct and non-committal; use it.

★ Why the bedtime snack works and why some facilities served one routinely: a half sandwich with milk, or graham crackers and milk, between 9 and 10 PM keeps the 2–3 AM glucose from bottoming out.

## Patient education (slides 31–32)

**Certified Diabetic Educators (CDE)** exist, but many facilities have eliminated the role — ★ which means **the bedside nurse does the diabetic education.**

- Overwhelming for the newly diagnosed
- **Difficult to re-educate patients who have developed poor habits**
- **Education is an ongoing, lifelong process**

**Basic skills to teach**
- Understanding of the **pathophysiology** — what is actually happening to the vessels, kidneys, eyes and nerves. Patients who think "sugar high, take insulin, done" do not change behavior.
- **Treatment modalities**
- **Recognizing signs of hypo- and hyperglycemia** — ★ **"Cold and clammy, need some candy. Hot and dry, sugar high."**
- **Practical knowledge** — how to actually do it

★★ **Two teaching principles she built stories around:**
1. **Never assume someone knows.** The mother given an antipyretic suppository who administered it **orally** because no one demonstrated it — the infant choked. Explain everything, and get a **return demonstration** for any skill.
2. **A title is not knowledge.** The **ER nurse** who ran out of insulin over a holiday, kept eating, went to bed feeling unwell, and was found unresponsive the next morning in DKA. Teach every patient as though it is the first time they have heard it.

★ **Technique when the patient says "I know all this":** teach-back. *Tell me what you understand about diabetes. Tell me when you administer your medication. Tell me the signs and symptoms you watch for.* Then reinforce only the gaps.

★ Switching a patient from a **pen to a vial and syringe** is a new skill, not a refill — they must be taught to read the syringe, draw the correct amount, and inject, with return demonstration, before discharge.

### Ongoing education — preventing long-term complications (slide 32)

| Area | Content |
|---|---|
| **Foot care** | Inspect the **bottom of the feet daily** — neuropathy means they will not feel a pebble, a blister or a sore. Do not shave calluses. Care with toenail trimming (ingrown nail → infection). Routine **podiatrist** visits. ★ Untreated, these become **osteomyelitis** → 6 weeks of IV antibiotics via PICC, or amputation |
| **Eye care** | Routine ophthalmology — damage is silent and irreversible once present |
| **General hygiene / skin care** | Prevent **dryness**, because dry skin cracks and every break is an entry point for infection; moisturize; check for cuts and abrasions |
| **Oral hygiene** | Included on the slide |
| **Risk factor management** | **Blood pressure, weight, cholesterol** |
| **Alternative "gadgets"** | CGM operation; newer combined pump/CGM systems |

⊙ Sick-day management is explicitly deferred to Part 2 — ★ but she flagged it as critical for type 1: **take the insulin even on sick days.**

## Appendix — delivered in this session, belongs to the Part 2 deck

She ran past the Part 1 deck into type 2 and nutrition, and stopped at glycemic index. This content is in the transcript but **not in the Part 1 slides** — it is Lecture 3 scope. Captured here so it isn't lost; the flashcards for it come with the Part 2 deck.

**Type 2 diabetes:** insulin resistance at the cellular level and/or impaired insulin secretion; **progressive, gradual onset**; **95%** of diabetes, and **80% of those have obesity**. Risk factors: genetics, obesity, ethnicity, lifestyle. Presentation is often a complication rather than the 3 Ps — **recurrent infections** (bacteria thrive on sugar), **blurred vision**, **fatigue**, **pruritus** (itching), **paresthesia** (tingling/numbness), **slow-healing wounds**. Treatment: diet and exercise first, then oral agents, then insulin; if caught early enough, nutrition therapy can bring type 2 glucose back down, and some patients come off medication — type 1, being autoimmune, never resolves.

**Nutrition therapy (ADA):** carbohydrates **50–60%** of total daily calories, favoring whole grains, brown rice, whole wheat, non-starchy vegetables, low glycemic index · fat **< 30%**, saturated **< 10%** · protein **10–20%** · fiber **25–35 g daily** (soluble lowers glucose and cholesterol; insoluble supports digestion and satiety). **Exchange lists — six categories:** starch, meat, fat, vegetables, fruits, free foods; **one starch serving ≈ 15 g carbohydrate**. Insulin dosing runs off carbs: **1–2 units of rapid-acting per 15 g of carbohydrate.**

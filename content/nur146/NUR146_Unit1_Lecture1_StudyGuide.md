## 1.1 Endocrine vs. exocrine

| | Endocrine gland | Exocrine gland |
|---|---|---|
| Secretes into | Bloodstream, directly | Ducts |
| Reaches | Any cell with a receptor for that hormone | Epithelial surface or GI tract |
| Examples | Pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, adrenal, pancreatic islets, ovaries, testes | Sweat, tears, saliva, breast milk, digestive juices |

- Sometimes an endocrine gland is also an organ; sometimes it is *only* a gland. ★ (transcript)
- Exocrine examples she named: sebaceous, mammary, sudoriferous (sweat)

## 1.2 Tissues that make hormones but are not endocrine glands ⊙

Hormones are *generally* produced by endocrine glands but also by specialized tissue:
- **GI mucosa** → gastrin, enterogastrone, secretin, cholecystokinin → digestion
- **Kidneys** → erythropoietin → stimulates bone marrow to make RBCs
  - *Why it matters:* dialysis/CKD patients lose this → they receive epoetin injections ★ (her clinical example)
- **WBCs** → cytokines (hormone-like proteins) → inflammatory and immune responses

## 1.3 Roles of the endocrine system (slide 7)

Fluid and electrolyte balance · Metabolism · Growth · Reproduction · Aging · Response to stress or other adverse conditions

⊙ Textbook adds: transport of chemicals across cell membranes, acid–base balance, adaptation

## 1.4 Hormones — what they are (slide 8)

- Chemical transmitter
- Released in **small amounts** from glands
- Transported via bloodstream to target cells or organs
- Transfer information and instructions from one set of cells to another
- Maintain homeostasis via the **negative feedback system**

**Hormone vs. neurotransmitter** ★
- Hormones = chemical transmitters, act **slowly**; neurotransmitters act **fast**
- They work in tandem — the endocrine system works in concert with the nervous system
- ⊙ Some chemicals do both: epinephrine is a neurotransmitter that functions as a hormone when needed

**Target tissue** = the cell that has receptors for that specific hormone. No receptor, no effect.
- Widespread effect example: thyroid hormone (metabolism in all tissues)
- Limited effect example: FSH, MSH

⊙ **How hormones act on the cell** (textbook, not on the slides)
- Peptide/protein/amine hormones → bind receptors **on the cell surface** → stimulate adenyl cyclase → ↑ cyclic AMP → cAMP is the **"second messenger"** → alters enzyme activity. Acts in seconds to minutes.
- Steroid hormones → small and lipid-soluble → **penetrate the cell membrane**, bind intracellular receptors → modify mRNA formation → protein synthesis. Takes **several hours**.
- ⊙ **Paracrine** action = hormone acts locally where released, never enters bloodstream (sex hormones on ovaries). **Autocrine** action = hormone acts on the cell that released it (insulin on pancreatic beta cells).

## 1.5 Hormone classification by structure ⚠

**She taught seven groups** (lecture only — not on the slides):

| Group | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Amines | Derived from a single amino acid | Epinephrine, norepinephrine, thyroid hormones |
| Peptides | Chain of amino acids; act at the cell surface | TRH, MSH, GH |
| Polypeptides | "Poly" = many | ADH (9 amino acids), oxytocin (9 amino acids) |
| Proteins | Large chains | Insulin (51 aa), GH (191 aa), prolactin (198 aa) |
| Glycoproteins | | TSH |
| Steroids | Built from cholesterol; produced by the adrenal cortex; **essential for life**; penetrate the cell membrane and act inside the cell | Cortisol, corticosteroids, testosterone, estrogen, progesterone |
| Fatty acids | | Eicosanoids, retinoids (vision, immune regulation) |

⚠ **Conflict.** The textbook gives **four** categories, not seven: (1) amines and amino acids; (2) **peptides, polypeptides, proteins, and glycoproteins** as one combined group; (3) steroids; (4) fatty acid derivatives. Her seven is the textbook's four with group 2 broken out. **Exam answer = her seven**, but if a question asks "how many categories," the textbook says four. Worth asking her which she'll test.

⚠ She placed GH under both peptides and proteins in the same explanation. The textbook lists GH with the peptide/protein group, so either is defensible — don't lose time on it.

## 1.6 Tropic vs. non-tropic hormones (slide 9) ★

| | Tropic | Non-tropic |
|---|---|---|
| Target | **Other endocrine glands** | Cells, tissues, organs |
| Effect | Stimulate production and secretion of hormones by another endocrine gland | Stimulate cellular growth, metabolism, other functions |
| Examples | TSH, LH, MSH, ACTH, FSH | Thyroxine, cortisol |

**Why:** a tropic hormone is a middle manager — it tells another gland to work. A non-tropic hormone does the work itself on tissue.

## 1.7 What causes hormones to be released? (slide 10)

- Stress
- Nervous system
- **Decreasing blood levels of the hormone** (= the negative feedback loop)
- Tropic (regulatory) hormones — target cells sit on other endocrine glands; release of one results in release of another (TSH, FSH, LH, ACTH)

## 1.8 Negative feedback ★★ (slide 11)

**Goal: HOMEOSTASIS.**

Blood glucose ↑ → pancreas releases **insulin** (promotes glucose uptake) → blood glucose drops → pancreas releases **glucagon** (promotes glycogen breakdown) → blood glucose rises → back to insulin.

- Hormone concentration ↑ → further production is **inhibited** ⊙
- Hormone concentration ↓ → rate of production **increases** ⊙
- Stopping point = when homeostasis is reached, not when the stimulus disappears

**Her thermostat analogy** ★ (she spent real time on this — expect it as a stem)
Set the house to 74°. Too hot → AC kicks on. Too cold → heat kicks on. Reach 74° → both shut off. That shut-off at the set point is what makes it *negative* feedback.

## 1.9 Positive feedback (slide 12)

- As long as the **stimulus is present**, the action of the hormone continues
- Release of a hormone initiates actions that lead to **additional release of that same hormone**
- **Goal is NOT homeostasis** ★
- Primary examples on the slide: **oxytocin, blood clotting**
- Cervical stretching → oxytocin → stronger contractions → more stretching (the loop on the slide)
- Her example: breastfeeding — as long as the baby latches, milk is produced; the only thing that stops it is removal of the stimulus
- ⚠ In the transcript she attributed the breastfeeding loop to **prolactin**. The textbook assigns **oxytocin** to milk ejection and prolactin to milk production. The slide's stated example is oxytocin. Go with oxytocin on the exam.
- Nearly every disorder in this unit involves the **negative** feedback system; positive feedback gets minimal coverage ★

## 1.10 Hypothalamus

- Location: brain, above the pituitary
- Secretes hormones involved in fluid balance and smooth muscle contraction
- Controls secretions of the **anterior pituitary** via releasing factors
- ⊙ Releasing factors reach the anterior pituitary through a special circulation, the **pituitary portal blood system**
- Produces ADH and oxytocin, which are **stored** in the posterior pituitary

## 1.11 Pituitary gland (slides 20–22) ★

- AKA **hypophysis**, the **"master gland"**
- Located on the **inferior aspect of the brain**; divided into **anterior** and **posterior** lobes
- Controlled by the hypothalamus
- Secretes **tropic** hormones that affect other endocrine glands
- ⊙ About **1.27 cm (½ inch)** in diameter — she described it as "the size of a pea," responsible for an enormous amount
- ⊙ Connected to the hypothalamus by the **pituitary stalk**

**Memory strategy she gave** ★: learn the **two** posterior hormones; everything else is anterior.

| Anterior pituitary (6 major) | Posterior pituitary (2 major) |
|---|---|
| **FSH** — follicle-stimulating hormone | **Vasopressin / ADH** — antidiuretic hormone |
| **LH** — luteinizing hormone | **Oxytocin** |
| **Prolactin** — acts on breast to stimulate milk production ⊙ | |
| **ACTH** — adrenocorticotropic hormone | |
| **TSH** — thyroid-stimulating hormone | |
| **GH** — growth hormone (somatotropin) ⊙ | |

- Anterior: secretion controlled by **releasing factors from the hypothalamus**; hormones released into circulation and act on specific target organs
- Posterior: hormones are **produced in the hypothalamus and stored in the posterior pituitary** ★ (classic test point)
- ⊙ Main function of TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH = release of hormones from other endocrine glands (i.e., they are the tropic ones)
- ⊙ Also secretes melanocyte-stimulating hormone (melanin production → skin color, freckles) and beta-lipotropin (function poorly understood)
- ⊙ **GH** is the most abundant anterior pituitary hormone: regulates growth in children, energy and metabolism in adults; ↑ protein synthesis, ↑ fatty acid breakdown, ↑ blood glucose. Increased by deep sleep, stress, exercise, fasting, malnutrition, hypoglycemia, trauma, hypovolemic shock, sepsis. Decreased by obesity, depression, hypothyroidism.
- ⊙ **Vasopressin/ADH** controls water excretion by the kidney; secretion stimulated by ↑ blood osmolality or ↓ blood pressure
- ⊙ **Oxytocin** facilitates milk ejection during lactation and increases force of uterine contractions in labor
- ⊙ Imbalanced **ACTH** = Addison disease (hypo) and Cushing syndrome (hyper) — coming in a later lecture

## 1.13 Thyroid (slides 23–24) ★

**Location:** anterior neck, below the cricoid cartilage; two lobes joined by the **isthmus**; sits behind the sternocleidomastoid muscles, anterior to the trachea

**Hormones produced**
- **T4 (thyroxine)** and **T3 (triiodothyronine)**
  - Affect growth, development, and metabolism of the body's cells
  - **Increase overall metabolic rate** in tissues
  - ⊙ T4 = **four** iodine atoms; T3 = **three** — the number in the name is the iodine count
  - Bound to **proteins until needed** (slide) — ⊙ the three binding proteins are thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), transthyretin, and albumin
- **Calcitonin**
  - **Reduces plasma calcium** → calcium is redeposited into bone
  - *Why:* calci-**tonin** "tones down" calcium. It is the mirror image of PTH.

**Iodine** ★
- Essential for the thyroid to synthesize its hormones
- Ingested in diet → absorbed via GI tract → thyroid concentrates iodide → converted to iodine → reacts with **tyrosine** to form thyroid hormones ⊙
- ⊙ Iodine deficiency → low circulating thyroid hormone → ↑ TSH → overproduction of thyroglobulin and **hypertrophy of the gland = goiter**

**Thyroid hormone regulation — the HPT axis** ★ (know the diagram)

Hypothalamus → **TRH** → anterior pituitary → **TSH** → thyroid → **T3 / T4** → ⊖ negative feedback back onto both the anterior pituitary and the hypothalamus

- *Why it matters:* this is the loop that makes TSH the screening test. Failing thyroid → low T3/T4 → less inhibition → **TSH rises**. That inverse relationship is the single most tested idea in the thyroid unit.
- ⚠ Student question in lecture: does the body release T4 and convert it to T3? Her answer was "some of it, yes," deferred to the thyroid lecture. Flag for follow-up — peripheral T4→T3 conversion is likely to come back in Lecture 2.

## 1.14 Parathyroid glands (slide 25) ★

- **Four** glands, on the **posterior aspect of the thyroid**, embedded in thyroid tissue ⊙
- **Separate endocrine glands** — not part of the thyroid, they just sit behind it ★
- Produce **parathormone (PTH)**
- Regulate **phosphorus and calcium** metabolism
- **↑ PTH → ↑ serum calcium**
- **↑ PTH → ↓ serum phosphorus**
- Negative feedback: ↑ serum ionized calcium → ↓ PTH secretion
- ⊙ PTH raises calcium by increasing absorption from **kidney, intestine, and bone**; its action is increased by the presence of **vitamin D**
- **Clinical clue she gave** ★: the usual way a parathyroid problem is found is an **elevated calcium** on a routine basic metabolic panel → prompts the provider to look at the parathyroid
- **Why it matters in renal patients:** PTH is central to calcium/phosphorus handling, so kidney disease and parathyroid disease travel together
- ⊙ When calcium × phosphorus rises, calcium phosphate can precipitate in organs (e.g. kidneys) → tissue calcification
- **Surgical risk she illustrated** ★: parathyroids can be embedded *within* thyroid tissue, so thyroidectomy can take them out with the thyroid — that is physical trauma causing secondary parathyroid dysfunction

## 1.15 Adrenal glands (slide 26)

- Located **above the kidneys**; AKA **suprarenal glands**
- **Two**, and they work **independently of each other** — one can be removed and the other does all the work ★
- Part of the **sympathetic** nervous system

| Adrenal **medulla** (inner) | Adrenal **cortex** (outer) |
|---|---|
| Releases **catecholamines**: epinephrine and norepinephrine | Responsible for **stress hormones** |
| **"Fight or flight"** | **Glucocorticoids** (cortisol) |
| | **Mineralocorticoids** (mineral/fluid balance) |
| | **Androgens** |

- Cortex hormones are involved in mineral balance, metabolic control, and resistance to stress
- Steroids from the adrenal cortex are **essential for life** ★

## 2.1 Endocrine imbalances (slide 13)

Hormones regulate **growth, development, mood, tissue function, metabolism, and sexual function** — so an imbalance can present in any of those domains.

Four imbalance mechanisms:
1. Hypo/hyper **production**
2. Hypo/hyper **secretion**
3. **Feedback disorders**
4. **Autoimmune conditions**

★ **Production vs. secretion — she answered this directly when a student asked:**
The gland can *produce* the hormone perfectly well, but if nothing is signaling it to *release*, the hormone stays put. Production is making it; secretion is releasing it. A failure of the signal (hypothalamus or pituitary) produces the same low serum level as a failure of the gland.

**Autoimmune condition** = the body attacks itself. Trigger is usually unknown; by the time signs and symptoms appear, it may have been going on a long time. ★

## 2.2 Primary vs. secondary gland dysfunction ★★ (slides 14–15)

**Primary = the problem originates in the gland itself.**

| Primary **hypersecretion** | Primary **hyposecretion** |
|---|---|
| Tumor (usually benign) | **Destructive** tumors |
| Idiopathic hyperplasia (overgrowth, cause unknown) | Inflammation |
| | Infection |
| | Autoimmune conditions |
| | Physical damage to the gland |

⚠ Note the same word, two directions: a tumor can *secrete* extra hormone (hyper) or *destroy* the gland (hypo). Questions exploit this.

**Secondary = changes in a gland caused by disease or treatment somewhere else.**
- May be **temporary or permanent**
- Result of factors **outside** the gland
- Result of **treatment of hyperfunction**: surgical removal of the gland · radiation therapy · pharmacotherapy
- Result of **treatment of hypofunction**: dietary changes · hormone replacement

**Worked examples she gave** ★
- Primary hypothyroidism = the problem is in the thyroid itself
- Secondary hyperthyroidism = an **adenoma on the pituitary** driving TSH, so the thyroid is being over-told
- Head trauma with a bleed pressing on the pituitary → pituitary dysfunction → downstream gland dysfunction
- Thyroidectomy that removes embedded parathyroids → secondary parathyroid dysfunction
- A treated hyperthyroid patient who changes their diet, or a diabetic whose insulin was raised and who then improves their intake → now overtreated

★ **Treating one direction can create the other.** Treat hypothyroidism and you can cause hyperthyroidism, and vice versa. Patient education about the signs of overshoot is part of the intervention.

## 2.3 History taking (slide 17)

Identify symptoms that can be manifestations of endocrine imbalance. **Seven on the slide:**
1. Energy level
2. Cold/hot tolerance
3. Unexplained weight changes
4. Thirst
5. Urine/bowel habits
6. Hair pattern changes
7. Menstrual cycle

⊙ The textbook's list adds: body proportions, muscle mass, fat and fluid distribution, secondary sexual characteristics, memory, concentration, sleep patterns, mood, vision, joint pain, sexual function.

⊙ Also document: **severity**, **how long** the change has been present, **effect on ADLs**, effect on **self-perception**, and **family history**.

★ **Her principle: these findings rule things in and rule things out — none of them is diagnostic alone.** Fatigue can be a hundred things.

## 2.4 Physical assessment (slide 18)

Identify specific **signs** that can indicate endocrine dysfunction:
- Vital signs — ★ **pain is the fifth vital sign** (speaker note, and she asked the class directly)
- Hair distribution
- **Facial hair in women**
- **Fat distribution** — asymmetric, not proportional
- Weight changes (+/−)
- **Exophthalmos** — abnormal protrusion of one or both eyeballs ⊙
- Edema
- **Truncal obesity**
- **Wasted extremities** — thin limbs compared with the rest of the body
- Fatigue

⊙ Textbook adds: head-to-toe inspection and **palpation of skin, hair, and thyroid**; round "moon face"; thinning skin; increased size of feet and hands; hyporeflexia or hyperreflexia; mood and behavior changes (nervousness, lethargy, fatigue); compare with previous findings when available.

⊙ **Thyroid examination technique:** inspect the lower neck between the sternocleidomastoid muscles for swelling or asymmetry → have the patient extend the neck slightly and swallow → thyroid tissue **rises with swallowing**. The **isthmus is the only part normally palpable**. If the gland is enlarged, **auscultate** both lobes with the diaphragm — a **bruit** indicates increased blood flow and suggests hyperthyroidism.

## 2.5 Diagnostic tests (slide 19) ★★

| Test | What it does | Used to detect |
|---|---|---|
| **Blood** | Measures level of circulating hormone, autoantibodies, and hormone effect on other substances (e.g. insulin on glucose) | Hypofunction or hyperfunction, and the **site** of dysfunction |
| **Urine** | Measures hormones or their **end products/metabolites** excreted by the kidneys; one-time or **24-hour** specimens | e.g. urinary free catecholamines in suspected pheochromocytoma ⊙ |
| **Stimulation test** | Give a stimulating hormone normally released by the hypothalamus or pituitary and watch whether the gland responds | **HYPOfunction** ★ |
| **Suppression test** | Give an exogenous dose of hormone and measure whether endogenous secretion shuts off | **HYPERfunction** ★ |

★ **The pairing to memorize: stimulation → hypo. Suppression → hyper.** Half the endocrine test questions in this course hang off it.

⊙ **Why stimulation localizes the problem:** if the gland *does* respond to the stimulating hormone, the defect is upstream in the **hypothalamus or pituitary**. If the gland *fails* to respond, the defect is **in the gland itself**. That is the primary-vs-secondary distinction turned into a test.

⊙ **Why suppression works:** it asks whether the organ is still obeying negative feedback. An organ that won't suppress isn't listening to the loop.

⊙ Disadvantages of urine testing: the patient may not be able to void on schedule, and medications or disease states can alter results.

⊙ **Imaging:** radioactive scanning, MRI, CT, ultrasonography, PET, dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry.

⊙ **Genetic screening** is becoming routine — DNA testing identifies genes associated with endocrine disorders and predisposition.

★ Nursing role in diagnostics: educate the patient about the **purpose** of the study, **what to expect**, and **possible side effects** before testing; note **trends** in results, which show disease progression and response to therapy.

## Objective 3 — Management strategies for endocrine disorders

Lecture 1 covers only the framework; specific regimens belong to Lectures 2+.

**Treating hyperfunction**
- Surgical removal of the gland
- Radiation therapy
- Pharmacotherapy
- → each of these can produce **permanent hypofunction**, which is secondary dysfunction by definition

**Treating hypofunction**
- Dietary changes
- Hormone replacement

★ **Her framing of the trade-off** (from the Graves disease example): when a gland can't be regulated after years of medication, the decision becomes *which* set of harms to accept — lifelong hormone replacement versus the adverse effects of long-term antithyroid medication. Hormone replacement is not a cure; it is a substitution with its own risks.

★ Patient education is an intervention, not an afterthought: what the condition is, which signs and symptoms to watch for, and what over- or under-replacement feels like.

## Objective 4 — Use the nursing process as a framework (slide 16)

| Step | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **Assessment** | Health history · physical assessment |
| **Analysis** | Formulate the nursing diagnosis · identify the most pressing issues |
| **Planning** | Set goals; re-plan when the situation changes |
| **Intervention** | Patient education · medications |
| **Evaluation** | Did it work? What needs to change? |

★ **Assessment is not just the physical exam — it is asking the questions.**

★ **Do not assume the person before you asked the questions.** Her case: a 54-year-old admitted from the ED as a UTI causing confusion. UTIs absolutely cause confusion, but typically in **older adults**, because normal physiologic changes of aging blunt the usual UTI symptoms so it goes unrecognized longer. Confusion from UTI in a 54-year-old is not the expected picture. One question to the husband — *how long has this been going on?* — got the answer **three months**. Three months of confusion is not a UTI. She called the attending, the patient was referred to neurology for a workup, and never should have been admitted.

★ **Patients don't know what they don't know.** They know something is wrong but not what it means. "I can't breathe" covers enormous ground. A patient will say they have no blood pressure problem and then hand you a bottle of lisinopril — because in their mind the medication *fixed* it. Ask about medications and ask why they take each one.

★ **Evaluation closes the loop:** was what we did effective, and do I need to change it?

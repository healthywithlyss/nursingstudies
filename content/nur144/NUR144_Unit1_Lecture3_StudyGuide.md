## PART ONE — THE BILIARY SYSTEM

### The river picture

The liver makes bile and the pancreas makes enzymes. Each flows down small **tributaries** into a shared main channel to the duodenum.

> **Block that channel low down and EVERYTHING upstream backs up.**

That one image explains why a single stone at the ampulla causes **jaundice AND pancreatitis at once** — both organs drain through the same outlet.

### The ducts, in order

**Common HEPATIC duct** → **GALLBLADDER** (storage) → out through the **CYSTIC duct** → **COMMON BILE duct** → **duodenum**

### The gallbladder

- **Absorbs water and minerals**, concentrating bile **5–10 times**
- Capacity **30–50 mL**
- Thick wall, attached at the mid-portion undersurface of the liver
- Contracts in response to **CHOLECYSTOKININ (CCK)**, a hormone secreted by the **small intestine**

> **Why stones form here:** concentrate any solution enough and the solids **precipitate out** — like a glass of salt water left to evaporate, leaving crystals behind. The same reason the **kidneys** form stones.

> **LITH** means stone anywhere: chole**lith**iasis, nephro**lith**iasis, and a **feca**lith** — a stone of stool, the main cause of appendicitis.

### The pancreas — exocrine function

**Production and release of digestive enzymes** for protein, fat and carbohydrate.
**Triggered by bile.** **Activated in the DUODENUM** — not in the pancreas itself.

> That last point is the whole of pancreatitis. The enzymes are supposed to activate *downstream*. When they can't get out, they activate *inside*.

### The small intestine

Three parts: **DUODENUM · JEJUNUM · ILEUM**
Deactivates **pancreatic enzymes** and **stomach acid**.

---

### Labs and diagnostics

**Labs ordered for biliary and pancreatic dysfunction:**
- **Pancreatic enzymes** — AMYLASE and LIPASE
- **Liver function tests** — AST, ALT
- **Bilirubin** — direct and indirect

| Lab | Points to |
|---|---|
| **ALT** | The **liver** |
| **ALP** (alkaline phosphatase) | **Bile flow** problems |
| **Lipase** | The **pancreas** — specific |
| **Amylase** | Pancreas, but **also salivary glands** |
| **WBC** | Inflammation — up in cholecystitis |

**"A before L"** — amylase rises first, lipase is more specific.

**In acute pancreatitis:** both elevated within **24 hours** of onset. **Amylase returns to normal in 48–72 hours. Lipase stays elevated longer.**

**Five diagnostic studies for the biliary system:**
Ultrasound · CT · MRI · Endoscopy · **ERCP**

**Textbook additions:**

- **CHOLESCINTIGRAPHY (HIDA scan)** — a radioactive agent given IV, taken up by hepatocytes, excreted through the biliary tract, then scanned. Used for acute cholecystitis or duct blockage **when ultrasound is inconclusive**, such as acalculous cholecystitis. *If the tracer never reaches the gallbladder, the cystic duct is blocked.*
- **PTC (percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography)** — dye injected **directly** into the biliary tract through skin and liver. **Distinguishes hepatocellular from obstructive jaundice.** Reserved for when ERCP is unsafe; mostly replaced by ERCP and MRCP.

### CHOLELITHIASIS — stones

**The most common abnormality of the biliary system.**

**Two types:**

| Type | Composition | Share | Key fact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PIGMENT** | Bilirubin + calcium salts | 20% | **Cannot be dissolved** — must be removed surgically |
| **CHOLESTEROL** | Cholesterol | 80% | Usually green |

**Risk factors by type:**

- **PIGMENT** — cirrhosis · **hemolysis** · biliary tract infection
  *(Anything producing excess bilirubin)*
- **CHOLESTEROL** — fatty diet · diabetes · heart disease · heredity · sedentary lifestyle · hypercholesteremia

**Who gets it:** classically a **woman in her 40s**, somewhat overweight. Plus **oral contraceptives** and **diabetes**.

> Estrogen raises cholesterol in bile — which is why both female sex *and* oral contraceptives increase risk.

**Presentation:**
Often **asymptomatic**. Otherwise **BILIARY COLIC**: severe RUQ pain with nausea and vomiting **several hours after a fatty meal**, radiating to the **back or right shoulder**.

> **Why a fatty meal:** fat releases **cholecystokinin**, which makes the gallbladder squeeze — against a stone.

> **Fever or a palpable mass** means it has progressed to obstruction, inflammation or infection.

**When a stone blocks the COMMON BILE DUCT — three findings:**
**JAUNDICE · DARK urine · LIGHT or CLAY-colored stool**

**Five approaches to medical management:**
ERCP · dietary management · **medications (ursodeoxycholic acid)** · **laparoscopic cholecystectomy** · nonsurgical removal by instrumentation or **lithotripsy** (intracorporeal or extracorporeal)

> **Ursodeoxycholic acid** is taken for **6–12 months** after diagnosis. It binds with bile and can dissolve small cholesterol stones — a slow process. (Same drug appeared in Lecture 2 as Actigall.)

---

### CHOLECYSTITIS — inflammation

| Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **CALCULOUS** | A **gallstone** obstructs bile outflow |
| **ACALCULOUS** | Inflammation **without** a stone |

**Presents:** RUQ pain with **rigidity**, **FEVER**, positive **MURPHY'S SIGN**, elevated **WBC**

**MURPHY'S SIGN** — press up under the **right costal margin** and ask the patient to **breathe in**. If the descending gallbladder hits your hand and they stop breathing from pain, it's positive. **About 97% specific** for cholecystitis.

> Inspiration pushes the diaphragm — and the inflamed gallbladder — down onto your fingers.
>
> *She flagged that students missed this on Kahoot because it isn't on the slides.*

**PRIORITY: NPO** — reduces gallbladder stimulation and inflammation.

> **Her rule:** *"For many GI things, NPO is usually a good answer. If you see it as an option, it is usually the right one."* Eating stimulates the organ you're trying to rest. And whenever a patient is NPO, think about **hydration**.

**Complications:** GANGRENE · PERFORATION · **secondary infection** of bile by enteric organisms. Chronic may include **EMPYEMA**.

> **Cholecystitis is inflammation. Cholelithiasis is stones.** You can have stones with no inflammation, and inflammation with no stones.

### Biliary nursing care

**Seven assessments:** carefully collect patient **history** · assess **learning needs** · consider **respiratory status** and risk factors (especially post-op) · assess **nutritional status** · monitor for **bleeding** dysfunction · assess **GI symptoms** · assess for post-op complications

**Five nursing diagnoses:** acute pain · impaired **gas exchange** · impaired **skin integrity** · imbalanced nutrition · deficient knowledge

> **Gas exchange** because the incision is high and near the diaphragm, so patients splint and under-ventilate.
> **Skin integrity** because bile is caustic and there may be a drainage system.

**Seven goals:** relief of pain · adequate **ventilation** · intact **skin** · improved **biliary drainage** · optimal nutritional intake · absence of complications · understanding **self-care** routines

**Seven post-operative interventions:**
**Low Fowler** position · **NG or NPO until bowel sounds return**, then a **soft, low-fat, high-carbohydrate** diet · care of the biliary drainage system · analgesics · **turn, cough and deep breathe with SPLINTING** · ambulation · self-care education

**After laparoscopic surgery, assess five things:**
Loss of appetite (anorexia) · vomiting · pain · distention · fever
→ **They suggest INFECTION or DISRUPTION OF THE GI TRACT.**

**Self-care teaching after lap chole (textbook):**
Usually home the same day · resume normal activities within a week, **no heavy lifting** · **low-fat diet** initially, then advance · report **fever, worsening pain, jaundice, dark urine, pale stool, or drainage from incisions**

> **Shoulder pain from CO₂ insufflation** is common for a few days — walking helps. Gas irritating the diaphragm, the same phrenic nerve referral as perforation, but benign here.

**CHOLEDOCHOSTOMY (textbook)** — an incision into the **common bile duct** to remove stones, then a **T-tube** left in for gravity drainage until edema subsides. For a patient **too ill for cholecystectomy**; the lap chole is planned later.

**T-tube nursing care — five actions:**
Keep the drainage system **BELOW** the insertion site · **never clamp without an order** · observe and **record bile output** (amount, color, character) · assess skin for **bile leakage** and irritation · watch for obstruction: **pain, fever, jaundice, clay stools**

---

### After ANY GI surgery

The patient will almost always come back with an **NG tube to LOW INTERMITTENT SUCTION** — *"almost 90% of the time."*

**Why:** it **decompresses the stomach** so acid and digestive juices don't reach and disrupt the surgical site. Comes out a day or two later when bowel sounds return. Then NPO until bowel sounds, then **start low and go slow**.

**A STRICTURE** is a **narrowing** — the same thing as a **stenosis**. Fixed by placing a **stent** to hold it open or bypass it. Applies to any tube: pancreatic duct, bile duct, esophagus, ureter.

**Bile in vomit** is **greenish or yellowish**. It means contents from the **duodenum** are refluxing back, since bile enters at the ampulla of Vater, **below** the stomach. *If vomit contains bile, the obstruction is below the pylorus.*

## PART TWO — PANCREATITIS

### ACUTE PANCREATITIS

**Mechanism:** the pancreatic duct becomes **OBSTRUCTED**, so enzymes cannot get out and begin **DIGESTING THE PANCREAS ITSELF**.

**Cause:** **cholelithiasis or alcohol account for 80%.**

**Two forms:**
- **INTERSTITIAL EDEMATOUS** — mild, self-limiting
- **NECROTIZING** — severe, resistant to treatment

**Pain:** SEVERE, **MIDEPIGASTRIC**, onset **24–48 hours** after a heavy meal or alcohol. Diffuse, then localizing. **Worse after meals.** With guarding.

> **THE TWO NEGATIVES — this is the discriminator:**
> **NOT relieved by antacids** (it isn't an acid problem)
> **NOT relieved by vomiting** (emptying the stomach doesn't unblock the pancreatic duct — unlike an ulcer, where vomiting relieves)

**Also:** nausea and vomiting (may be **bile stained**), fever, jaundice, acutely ill appearance

### Diagnosis — 2 OF 3 required

1. History of upper abdominal pain
2. Serum **amylase or lipase greater than 3× the upper limit**
3. Abnormal imaging — CT, MRI or ultrasound

### SEVERE acute pancreatitis

**SHOCK signs:** mental **confusion** · hypotension, tachycardia, cold clammy skin, cyanosis · **RIGID BOARDLIKE abdomen** if peritonitis

**NAMED SIGNS:**
- **CULLEN** — bruising around the **UMBILICUS**
- **GREY TURNER** — bruising at the **FLANK**

> Both mean blood tracking from the **retroperitoneum** — the gland is bleeding. The pancreas is retroperitoneal, so blood tracks along tissue planes to the surface.

**ORGAN COMPLICATIONS:**
Acute **kidney injury** · **respiratory distress** with hypoxia and diffuse infiltrates · **HYPOCALCEMIA** · **HYPERGLYCEMIA** · **DIC**

**Why each:**
- **Third-spacing** drives the shock — inflamed tissue leaks fluid out of circulation, so the patient is hypovolemic while looking swollen
- **Hypocalcemia** — fat digested by escaped lipase **binds calcium** (saponification)
- **Hyperglycemia** — endocrine tissue damaged alongside the exocrine
- **DIC** — *"they clot, use up all their clots, and then they bleed."* Massive clotting consumes the clotting factors, then hemorrhage

### Treatment

**NPO** · feed by the **ENTERAL** route (parenteral only if enteral isn't tolerated) · **NG suction** to relieve vomiting and distention (**not routine**) · **analgesia** sufficient to minimize restlessness, often **opioids** · remove the blockage

**Diet when resumed:** **HIGH-carbohydrate, LOW-protein, LOW-fat**

> Food — especially **fat and protein** — triggers enzyme release, and enzyme release is what's digesting the organ. Carbohydrate stimulates it least.

**AVOID NSAIDs** — they affect **clotting**, and the patient may already be bleeding or heading toward DIC. *(Not primarily about stomach irritation.)*

> **Enteral over parenteral even here**, because feeding the gut preserves its integrity and lowers infection risk. Bypass the gut and it atrophies.

---

### CHRONIC PANCREATITIS

**Mechanism:** progressive inflammation where pancreatic cells are replaced by **FIBROUS TISSUE**.

> This is the pancreatic version of cirrhosis — working tissue replaced by scar. Fibrous tissue does not secrete.

**Cause:** most often **excessive alcohol** in the US; **malnutrition** worldwide

**Pain:** **RECURRENT attacks** of **upper abdominal AND BACK** pain with vomiting, so severe that even **opioids may not relieve it** (dependence risk). Attacks become **more frequent, more severe, longer**.

> **BACK pain** because the pancreas is **retroperitoneal** — the same reason a penetrating ulcer refers to the back.

**Lost function:**
- **Weight loss** and anorexia
- **STEATORRHEA** — frothy, frequent, foul *(no lipase, so fat passes undigested)*
- **Calcium stones** in the ducts in advanced disease

**Management:**
Prevent acute attacks · manage pain by the pain ladder
- **NONSURGICAL (2/3)** — endoscopy: stone removal, **stents** for strictures, cyst drainage
- **SURGICAL (1/3)** — **PANCREATICOJEJUNOSTOMY (Roux-en-Y)**: pancreatic secretions drain into the jejunum. Pain relief within 6 months for most. Also **Whipple** resection.

> The surgery makes a **new drainage route** — if the duct is blocked by fibrosis, bypass it into the bowel. Not a cure; many still have symptoms.

**ACUTE vs. CHRONIC:** acute is a sudden attack with fever, jaundice, confusion, agitation and ecchymosis, and it **can resolve**. Chronic is recurrent attacks with **progressive loss of function** — steatorrhea and weight loss — and the damage **does not reverse**.

---

### THE RIGID BOARDLIKE ABDOMEN

**Four conditions in this unit:** perforated **ULCER** · perforated **APPENDIX** · severe **PANCREATITIS** · ruptured **GALLBLADDER**

> **It always means PERITONITIS** — acid, enzymes or bacteria on the peritoneum. The muscles clamp involuntarily. A surgical emergency regardless of source.

## PART THREE — ALTERNATE NUTRITION

### Enteral vs. parenteral

| | **ENTERAL** | **PARENTERAL** |
|---|---|---|
| **Route** | Into the **GI tract** | Into a **vein**, bypassing the gut |
| **Requires** | A **functioning gut** | Used when the gut can't be used |
| **Main risks** | **Aspiration**, diarrhea, tube problems | **Catheter complications** and **SEPSIS** |

**Five advantages of enteral:**
Safe and cost effective · preserves **GI integrity** · preserves normal intestinal and hepatic metabolism · maintains fat metabolism and lipoprotein synthesis · maintains normal **insulin and glucagon ratios**

> Every advantage is the same idea: food arriving the normal way keeps the whole downstream system doing its job. **Bypass the gut and the gut atrophies.**

---

### Enteral routes

| Duration | Routes | When |
|---|---|---|
| **TEMPORARY** | Nasogastric · nasoduodenal · nasojejunal | Stomach bypassed for **less than 4 weeks**, or high **aspiration** risk. Typically under 12-Fr |
| **LONG-TERM** | **Gastrostomy** (G-tube), including **PEG** · **Jejunostomy** (J-tube) | |

**A GJ tube** is a single tube with **two openings** — one into the **stomach**, one into the **jejunum**. Medications may be ordered to a specific port.

**G-tube sites over time:** every pull **stretches** the opening — like a pierced ear getting bigger. Eventually it **leaks**, and leakage causes **fungal infection** and skin breakdown. *(G-tubes rarely come out, because the reason it went in usually doesn't go away.)*

---

### Feeding methods

**BOLUS** — total daily volume divided into **4–6 feeds**, typically **200–400 mL** each. Given by **syringe** (over 50 mL) by **GRAVITY** over 15–60 minutes. Start slowly.

> **THE GRAVITY RULE:** *"When it is HIGHER than you, it goes INTO you. When it is LOWER than you, YOU go into it."*
>
> **Raise** the syringe to speed up, **lower** it to slow down. Her ER story: a patient set his IV bag on the bathroom floor and it filled with blood. Same physics.

**CONTINUOUS or CYCLIC** — over **8–18 hours**, **pump** controlled. For **critically ill** patients, **high aspiration risk**, risk for intolerance, and **small bowel placements**.

> Small bowel placements **must** be continuous — the jejunum has no reservoir, so a bolus there causes **dumping syndrome**.

**DUMPING SYNDROME** — a concentrated feeding hits the small intestine too fast, and **water is pulled out of the bloodstream into the bowel** to dilute it. *Same third-spacing physics as ascites.*

---

### Formulas

**Six things vary:** caloric density · **osmolality** · fiber · minerals · electrolytes · cost

> **ALL of them lack the full amount of water needed** — formula is only **70–85% free water**.

**Treat formula like a medication.** There are many types — Peptamen, Peptamen Junior, high-calorie, vegetarian — and each patient is prescribed a specific one. **Check it the way you check a drug.**

---

### Administration

**POSITION** — **Semi-Fowler, head elevated AT LEAST 30 DEGREES**, during and after

**PLACEMENT CHECK:**
| Method | Reliability |
|---|---|
| **X-RAY** | **GOLD STANDARD** |
| Aspirate and check pH (acidic) | Good |
| Auscultate 5 mL of air | **Most common, LEAST reliable** |

Also check the **external marking** every time, to detect drift.

> Nasoduodenal and nasojejunal tubes have a **metal tip** precisely so they can be x-rayed — placement beyond the stomach must be confirmed.

**WATER** — flush **before and after each medication**, **before and after each feeding**, **every 4–6 hours**, and **whenever feeding is stopped or interrupted**

> **HER ROOKIE MISTAKE:** she turned off a pump for an NPO order and **did not flush**. By morning the tube was **jammed** with dried formula. Formula sets like glue.

**MEDICATIONS** — give **SEPARATELY**, never mixed with each other or with formula

**HANG TIME** — open system **no more than 4–8 hours**, to avoid bacterial contamination

---

### Assessment and monitoring

**BEDSIDE:** tube placement · head of bed above 30° · tolerance of formula and amount · signs of dehydration · signs of infection (**fever**)

**LABS:** BUN · serum protein · **PREALBUMIN** · electrolytes · kidney function · H&H · blood glucose. Plus I&O and weight.

> The labs answer two questions. **Is it working?** — prealbumin and serum protein (prealbumin responds within days). **Is it harming?** — electrolytes, glucose, BUN, kidney function.

**FLUID RETENTION signs:** elevated glucose · **decreased urine output** · **sudden weight gain** · periorbital or dependent **edema**

- **Skills checklist:** uses a **500 mL** threshold

### Enteral complications

| Complication | Cause |
|---|---|
| **ASPIRATION PNEUMONIA** | Improper tube placement, vomiting, lying flat |
| **DIARRHEA** | Hyperosmolar formula, rapid or bolus infusion, contamination |
| **TUBE OBSTRUCTION** | Inadequate flushing, poorly crushed medications |
| **CONSTIPATION** | Lack of fiber, inadequate fluid, immobility |
| **DEHYDRATION** | Hyperosmolar feeding with insufficient free water |
| **HYPERGLYCEMIA** | Glucose intolerance, high-carbohydrate formula |

> **Three of these trace to ONE fact:** formula is only 70–85% water. Too concentrated for the fluid given, and you get **diarrhea** (water pulled INTO the gut), **dehydration** (not enough free water), and **constipation** (not enough fluid to soften stool).

> **ASPIRATION is the one that kills.** Head of bed above 30°, confirm placement, and **STOP the feeding** at the first respiratory sign. Every response that continues feeding is wrong.

---

### PARENTERAL NUTRITION

**Four findings that identify a candidate:**
Decreased oral intake for **more than 1 week** · weight loss of **10% or more** of usual weight · **muscle wasting** and decreased tissue healing · **persistent** nausea and vomiting

**What's in it:** a nutrient-rich, sugar-rich solution — usually **DEXTROSE 10%**, with lipids (sometimes separate, sometimes combined into a milky yellow solution)

> So: **hyperglycemia risk**, and **"bacteria love it"** — a direct route for infection into a central line. Composition is set by a dietitian. **Checked like a medication** — it is one.

### Nursing care

**INFECTION**
- **Strict sterile technique**, dressing **every 3 days** per protocol
- *(Textbook: chlorhexidine disc under a semipermeable transparent dressing, changed **every 7 days** unless damp, bloody, loose or soiled. Occlusive gauze alternative every **48 hours**.)*
- **WEAR A MASK** — nurse and patient
- Assess the site **every shift**
- **Scrub the hub 15 seconds** before access; air-dry

> *"This is not like putting an IV in, this is a CENTRAL LINE."* It ends near the heart, so contamination goes straight to the systemic circulation. The **skin** and the **catheter hub** are the major sources of **CLABSI**.

**NUTRITION** — daily weight at the same time of day · accurate I&O · caloric count · trace elements

**GLUCOSE** — check regularly

**IF IT RUNS OUT OR STOPS SUDDENLY — hang D10** to prevent **REBOUND HYPOGLYCEMIA**

> The body has been producing insulin for a constant glucose load. Remove the load abruptly and the insulin is still there with nothing to act on.

**NEVER SPEED IT UP — REFEEDING SYNDROME**

A dangerous shift when nutrition is restarted **too rapidly** in a malnourished patient — cells suddenly take up electrolytes to metabolize the glucose.

**HYPOPHOSPHATEMIA, HYPOKALEMIA, HYPOMAGNESEMIA** shortly after infusion begins. Also nausea, vomiting, lightheadedness, abrupt vital sign changes.

> **Phosphate is the one that kills** — it's needed for ATP, and the heart runs out of energy.

> **Two opposite errors:** too fast **up** causes refeeding syndrome; too fast **down** causes rebound hypoglycemia.

HAI vs. Nosocomial & Source of Infection
HAI — Healthcare-Associated Infection

Infection contracted in ANY healthcare setting

Includes: nursing home, rehab, dialysis, urgent care, detox, home care, doctor's office

Greatest risk: chronically ill, elderly, pts on steroids, diabetics, immunocompromised

Impact: aggravate existing illness, lengthen stay & recovery, increase cost; one of the 10 leading causes of death in the US

Nosocomial — Hospital-Only

Infection acquired in the HOSPITAL only

Examples: CLABSI (central line), SSI (surgical site), CAUTI (catheter UTI), VAP (ventilator pneumonia), MDROs, C-Diff

C. difficile: most common & serious nosocomial; elderly on antibiotics = highest risk

Endogenous Infection

Source is inside the patient — normal flora altered (e.g., antibiotics disrupt gut flora → C. diff)

Exogenous Infection

Source is outside the patient — environmental organisms (e.g., staph from surfaces, hands, equipment)

Six Links — Chain of Infection (ALL 6 must be present for spread)
1 · Infectious Agent (Pathogen)

Bacteria, viruses, fungi, yeast, mold

Resident flora: live on/in us (intestines, skin) — normally harmless

Transient flora: temporary; removed by handwashing

Normal flora becomes pathogenic when it goes where it doesn't belong

2 · Reservoir

Where pathogens live & multiply

Living: humans, animals, insects — warm, dark, moist environments

Nonliving: food, soil, water, sinks, toilets, bed rails, overbed tables, linens, wound dressings, stethoscope

3 · Portal of Exit

How the pathogen leaves the reservoir

Body fluids: blood, mucus, urine, feces, breast milk, emesis, semen

Coughing, sneezing, saliva, wound drainage, tubes, IV lines

4 · Mode of Transmission

Contact — Direct: touching, kissing, sexual contact, wound drainage, scratch, bite

Contact — Indirect: fomite — contaminated object (stethoscope, thermometer, keyboard, pen, overbed table)

Droplet: water droplets when person exhales/coughs/sneezes/talks; suctioning & oral care

Airborne: aerosolized particles — travel >6 ft

Vector: ticks, fleas, mosquito, mites

5 · Portal of Entry

Normal openings: eye, nares, mouth, urethra, vagina, anus

Abnormal openings: wounds, cuts, scrapes, surgical incisions, IV/drainage tube sites, urinary catheters, bite from vector

Common in healthcare: wounds, surgical sites, urinary catheters, IV lines, GI tubes

6 · Susceptible Host

Person with inadequate defenses

Most susceptible: very old, very young, immunocompromised, immune deficiency

4 determining factors: virulence, organism's ability to survive in host, number of organisms, host's defenses

Break ANY link → stop the spread. Nursing interventions target every link: hand hygiene (agent/transmission), PPE (portal of exit/entry), isolation (reservoir), immunization (host).

Classifications of Infection

Local vs. Systemic

Local s/s: redness, edema, warm to touch, pain, drainage (if wound)

Systemic s/s: temp >100.4°F, tachycardia >100, WBC >10,000, fatigue

Untreated local → can become systemic

Acute vs. Chronic

Acute: rapid onset, short duration (common cold)

Chronic: slow development, long duration; may recur (osteomyelitis, abscess)

Latent

Present but no symptoms for long periods

Examples: HIV/AIDS, TB, shingles (herpes zoster)

Can reactivate when immune function drops

Body's Lines of Defense
PRIMARY (1st Line) — Physical & Anatomical Barriers

Normal Flora — protective competition

Skin (intact) — prevents pathogen entry

Respiratory tree — mucous membranes trap; nose hairs filter; cilia sweep upward; cough/sneeze expel

Eyes — lysozymes in tears

Mouth — lysozymes in saliva

GI tract — acidity, bile, vomiting, diarrhea

GU tract — mucous membranes, vaginal acidity, acid & lysozymes in urine

SECONDARY (2nd Line) — Chemical Activation When Pathogens Pass Primary

Phagocytosis

Special WBCs attack & kill pathogens — may see WBC >10,000

Complement Cascade

Blood proteins trigger chemicals to attack & rupture pathogen cell membranes; signals basophils → release histamine → inflammation; may see elevated basophils

Inflammation

Histamine from damaged cells → vessels dilate → redness, warmth, edema, pain

Fever

Raises core body temp → increases metabolism → inhibits pathogen growth; assess temp >100.4°F

TERTIARY (3rd Line) — Immune Memory (Antibodies)

ACTIVE — body makes its own antibodies/T-cells

  • Natural Active: got ill → body made antibodies (influenza, chicken pox)
  • Artificial Active: vaccine triggers antibody production (measles, mumps, polio, tetanus)

PASSIVE — antibodies received from another source

  • Natural Passive: placenta or breast milk
  • Artificial Passive: serum from another person/animal (rabies, botulism, hepatitis A immune globulin)

Types of Specific Immunity:

  • Specific immunity: immune system learns to ID & kill previously encountered pathogens; specific to lymphocytes
  • Humoral immunity: antibody response; circulates antibodies to kill pathogens; specific to WBC leukocytes
  • Cellular immunity: cell-mediated, no antibodies; specific to phagocytes, T & B cells; targets cancer, viruses, fungi, protozoans
Risk Factors for Infection (12)

Patient Characteristics

  • Age: very young (limited exposures, no immunizations); very old (↓ immune function, chronic illness, dehydrated skin)
  • Immunosuppressant drugs: chemo, biologics, steroids/prednisone, inhalers
  • Immune deficiency disease: HIV, autoimmune
  • Substance abuse: IV drugs, alcohol

Behaviors & Conditions

  • Lifestyle: unprotected risky behaviors, multiple partners, smoking, alcohol
  • Stress: physical or mental
  • Invasive surgical procedures
  • Improper antibiotic use: taking ABT for viral infections

Environmental & Physical

  • Living/work environment: homeless, no running water, coal mine
  • Skin breakdown: pressure injury = portal of entry
  • Inadequate nutrition: low albumin = no cell repair; dehydration
  • Travel outside the country
Activities That Promote Immune Function

Adequate Nutrition

Monitor food & fluid intake; protein, vitamins, minerals, water manufacture immune cells; replaces lost stores, maintains WBC production, repairs tissue; fever & ↑ mucus increase water loss

Balanced Hygiene

Teach hand hygiene; sufficient to decrease skin bacterial count; NOT overzealous — causes skin cracking, diminishes natural oils

Rest & Exercise

Educate 6–9 hrs sleep & regular exercise; renews body & mind, conserves strength

Reducing Stress

Physical or mental stress decreases immune defenses; laughing increases oxygenation, body movement, immune responses

Immunization

Encourage patients; protects against measles, mumps, childhood diseases, pneumonia, influenza, smallpox, shingles; ≥85% of population must be immunized to protect entire population

Asepsis — Medical vs. Surgical
Medical Asepsis — "Clean Technique"

Practices that REDUCE number of microorganisms & decrease spread

Microorganisms exist everywhere except on sterilized objects

Best method: frequent handwashing

Practices: hand hygiene, cleaning environment, disinfecting equipment between pts, wearing PPE, standard precautions, transmission-based isolation

Surgical Asepsis — "Sterile Technique"

Practices that DESTROY & ELIMINATE all microorganisms & spores

Sterilization: autoclave for surgical instruments; liquid chemicals for scoping

Critical nursing uses: inserting IV, urinary catheters, specific wound care, central line dressings, chest tubes

7 Surgical Asepsis Principles
#Principle
1Sterile object remains sterile only when touched by another sterile object
2Only sterile objects placed on a sterile field
3Sterile object/field out of vision OR held below waist = contaminated
4Sterile object/field becomes contaminated by prolonged exposure to air
5When sterile surface contacts a wet contaminated surface → contaminated by capillary action
6Fluid flows in the direction of gravity
7Edges of sterile field/container are contaminated — 1-inch border
Other sterile rules: Don't talk, laugh, sneeze, cough over a sterile field · Hold hands above elbows during surgical scrub (gravity) · Don't touch edge of container after removing sterile items · Never turn back on sterile field; never leave it unattended
Standard Precautions — 1st Tier (EVERY patient, ALL settings)

Assume ALL blood, body fluids, secretions, open skin, mucous membranes may contain pathogens.


When to Use What PPE

Gloves: anticipating contact with blood/body fluids; broken skin on hands; applying medication/lotion

Gown + eye protection: when splashing of urine, stool, emesis is anticipated

Also includes: safe injection practices; respiratory hygiene/cough etiquette; mask during lumbar puncture

DONNING PPE — "Bottoms Up" (Putting On)

  1. GOWN
  2. MASK
  3. GOGGLES / EYE SHIELD
  4. GLOVES

DOFFING PPE — "Alphabetical Order" (Taking Off)

  1. GLOVES
  2. GOGGLES
  3. GOWN
  4. MASK
Donning: Gown first (protect clothes), Gloves last (touch everything). Doffing: Gloves first (most contaminated), Mask last (protect face/airway).
Transmission-Based Precautions — 2nd Tier (ADDED to Standard)
CONTACT PRECAUTIONS — Direct or Indirect Contact

PPE: gloves & gown (eye protection if splashing)

Room: private OR cohort with like infections

Equipment: disposable OR clean/disinfect between pts; disinfect on removal from room

Supplies: double-bag linens, specimens, soiled supplies; limit transport

Examples (MDROs):

  • MRSA (methicillin-resistant staph aureus)
  • VRE (vancomycin-resistant enterococcus)
  • ESBL (extended spectrum beta-lactamase)
  • C-Diff (Clostridium difficile)

C-Diff SPECIAL: Highly contagious via spores · Requires BLEACH for surfaces · Use SOAP & WATER (NOT alcohol foam) · Often nosocomial from antibiotic use

DROPLET PRECAUTIONS — Large Particles >5 Microns, Travel 3–6 Feet

Source: cough, sneeze, talk, suctioning

PPE: surgical mask

Room: private OR cohort

Transport: patient wears surgical mask; disinfect equipment on removal

Examples:

  • Influenza (flu)
  • Pertussis (whooping cough)
  • Meningitis
  • Mumps
  • Measles (Rubella/German measles)
  • Strep throat
AIRBORNE PRECAUTIONS — Aerosolized Particles Travel >6 Feet

Source: resp secretions, coughing, resp treatments, suctioning, BiPAP, AC, sweeping, fans, shaking linens

PPE: N95 respirator (PAPR if not fit-tested) — must be FIT TESTED

Room: private NEGATIVE PRESSURE room ALONE; 6–12 air exchanges/hr; doors closed

Transport: limit; pt wears SURGICAL mask (not fit-tested for N95); disinfect equipment on removal

Examples — MTV + C SARS:

  • Measles (Rubeola)
  • Tuberculosis
  • Varicella (chicken pox); open shingles (Herpes Zoster — disseminated = wider spread)
  • COVID
  • SARS (coronaviruses)
PROTECTIVE ENVIRONMENT — Reverse / Neutropenic Isolation

For IMMUNOCOMPROMISED patients — to PROTECT THEM from organisms

PPE for staff & visitors: hand hygiene, gloves, gown, mask; signage; individual equipment OR disinfect ALL equipment before bringing in

Restrict: sick visitors (even common cold); limit transport; transporters wear PPE

PROHIBITED in room:

  • NO fresh fruits, salads, or vegetables
  • NO live plants or fresh flowers
  • NO standing water
  • NO invasive procedures
ALWAYS assess how the patient is coping with isolation — it is a frightening and unknown experience.
Nursing Interventions — Medical & Surgical Asepsis + Supporting Host Defenses

Infection Prevention Actions

  • Hand hygiene before & after patient contact
  • Soap & water at shift start/end, C-Diff, visibly soiled hands
  • Medical & surgical asepsis (disinfect environment & equipment; teach pt/visitors handwashing)
  • Appropriate precautions & PPE (only transmissible infections & reverse/protective iso require private room)
  • Administer ordered meds (antibiotics q8h, fever, pain)
  • Monitor V/S q4h; monitor lab values daily

Supporting Host Defenses

  • Maintain skin integrity (T&P q2h; keep dry if incontinent)
  • Reduce stress/anxiety (explain isolation/procedures/treatments; allow rest; keep informed; therapeutic communication)
  • Promote immune function: diet, activity, sleep, lifestyle
  • Encourage immunizations

12 Factors That Affect Skin Integrity

1. Age

Older adults: ↓ sebaceous/sweat glands (dry skin), thinning subQ layer, weakened epidermis-dermis bond, slower healing, chronic disease interferes with healing

2. Impaired Mobility

Body weight → pressure → breakdown; altered sensory perception, paralysis, sedation, disease processes limiting mobility

3. Nutrition

Protein to maintain/repair/preserve intravascular volume; ALBUMIN level; adequate caloric intake; ascorbic acid, zinc, copper for wound healing

4. Hydration

Both over AND under cause breakdown: Edema = poor microcirculation, ↓ O2/nutrients; Dehydration = no elasticity, low fluid/blood flow

5. Diminished Sensation

Diabetics, PVD, stroke, spinal cord injury, fractures — difficulty feeling hot/cold/pain; pressure from ill-fitting clothing/shoes

6. Diminished Cognition

Alzheimer's, dementia — unaware of need to reposition; higher risk for breakdown

7. Impaired Circulation

Main cause of chronic wounds; arterial impairs O2/nutrient delivery; venous causes edema & stasis with waste products

8. Medication

Pruritus, rashes, photosensitivity, pigmentation changes; BP meds, NSAIDs, steroids (inhibit healing), anticoagulants (heparin, warfarin), chemotherapeutic agents

9. Moisture

Causes maceration (softening of skin); incontinence & fever are most common causes

10. Fever

Sweating = maceration; ↑ metabolic rate raises tissue O2 demand

11. Infection

Microorganism present, wound contamination, ↑ metabolic demands, makes skin more vulnerable, impedes healing

12. Lifestyle

Tanning, bathing too much/little, diet, smoking, piercings, tattoos

Classification of Wounds — 4 Ways
By Skin Integrity

Open: break in skin or mucous membrane

Closed: no break — bruise, edema, hematoma

By Length of Time for Healing

Acute: short, no complications; all 3 healing phases occur (inflammation, proliferation, maturation)

Chronic: longer to heal due to interrupted healing — infection, trauma, edema, ischemia; pressure ulcers, diabetics, corticosteroids, PAD, PVD

By Level of Contamination

Clean: no infection, limited inflammation = LOW infection risk

Clean contaminated: surgical incisions of GI/resp/GU = INCREASED risk

Contaminated: open wounds, pressure ulcers, trauma, surgeries with break in asepsis

Infected: bacteria >100,000 per gm tissue; s/s = REEDA & COCAF + pain

By Depth of Wound

Superficial: epidermis only

Partial thickness: epidermis + dermis

Full thickness: through dermis into subcutaneous tissue

Penetrating: through to underlying organs/cavity

Types of Wound Healing

Regenerative / Epithelial

Epidermis & dermis regeneration; partial thickness wounds

Primary Intention

Clean, approximated edges = surgical wound; minimum scarring; LEAST infection risk

Secondary Intention

Greatest tissue loss, wide wound base; heals from base by filling with granulation tissue inside-out; NOT approximated; example = pressure ulcer

Tertiary Intention (Delayed Closure)

Open wound, can't close incision; allow secondary intention healing first, then suture once no s/s of infection/inflammation

Phases of Wound Healing
1. Inflammatory (Cleansing) — 1–5 days

Hemostasis: vessels constrict, platelets aggregate to slow bleeding, clotting mechanism activates

Inflammation: edema, erythema, pain, ↑ temp, WBCs to area; macrophages do phagocytosis; scab forms

2. Proliferative (Granulation) — 5–21 days

HIGHER risk of infection here due to time

Cells develop & grow to fill from base

Granulation tissue: fibroblasts, new blood/lymph vessels — beefy red, bleeds easily

Epithelialization: epithelial cells grow from edges, seal wound

3. Maturation (Remodeling) — 14–21 days → 3–6 months

Collagen fibers broken down & reorganized

Increases tensile strength of wound

Scar tissue formation

Complications of Wound Healing

Hemorrhage

Rapid blood loss, severed/injured vessel; know pt history — bleeding/clotting disorder, anticoagulants

Infection

Microorganisms present; wound not healing; REEDA/COCAF + systemic (fever, ↑ WBC)

Dehiscence

Rupture/separation of wound layers (NOT total)

Evisceration

Total separation with protrusion of viscera through wound — SURGICAL EMERGENCY

Fistula Formation

Abnormal passage connecting two body cavities; d/t infection/debris → pressure → abnormal passageway; GI & GU tracts

REEDA — Surgical Wound Assessment

  • R = Redness
  • E = Edema
  • E = Ecchymosis
  • D = Drainage
  • A = Approximation

Surgical Wound Care Notes

  • Surgeon changes the 1st dressing
  • Surgical dressings are sterile
  • If original dressing soiled: mark outline of drainage; reinforce with dry sterile gauze; report COCAF to surgeon
  • Assess: REEDA, count sutures/staples/steri-strips, document drain length
Pressure Injuries — Definition & Staging

Definition: Localized injury to skin & underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence. Pressure → tissue ischemia → tissue cell death. Can occur in as little as 2 hours.

Reactive Hyperemia: Start of ischemia — skin intact; pressure causes pale/cool skin; when pressure relieved → vasodilation → red. If redness doesn't disappear quickly = tissue damage = NONBLANCHABLE.


Intrinsic Factors (Internal — Patient Health)

  • Chronic illness (diabetes, medications such as corticosteroids)
  • Impaired circulation (diabetes, atherosclerosis, low BP)
  • Reduced O2 supply in blood (tobacco users, anemic)
  • Limited mobility or reduced sensation (nerve damage, head injury, stroke, SCI, contractures)
  • Impaired cognition (Alzheimer's, dementia, altered LOC)
  • Plus: immobility, poor nutrition, edema, age, fever/sepsis

Extrinsic Factors (External — Environment)

  • Friction: skin rubbing against surface
  • Shearing: pt sliding downward; epidermis and dermis separate
  • Moisture: incontinence, sweat
  • Compression: over bony prominences AND under medical devices (ET tubes, casts, catheters)
Healing ulcers are NOT reverse-staged — a stage 4 healing wound stays documented as "Stage 4: healing." Never downgrade the stage.

Braden Scale

Tool to assess skin impairment & pressure injury risk. LOWER score = MORE likely to develop pressure injury.

6 Categories

  1. Sensory Perception
  2. Moisture
  3. Activity
  4. Mobility
  5. Nutrition
  6. Friction & Shear

Score Thresholds

≤18 = at risk for skin breakdown

<12 = high risk

When: every shift / every 8 hours (per facility policy)

Interventions — Prevention

  • Inspect skin daily; q1–2h if at greater risk
  • T&P q2h
  • Manage moisture (incontinence care, barrier cream)
  • Lotion & massage — do NOT massage over bony prominences
  • Monitor labs, nutrition, hydration
  • Pressure-limiting & positioning devices
  • Side-lying: prevent direct pressure on trochanter
  • HOB at lowest degree consistent with condition
  • Use Hoyer lift when possible
  • Totally relieve pressure on heels (raise off bed)
Heat & Cold Therapy
Heat Therapy

Effects: vasodilation · ↑ capillary permeability · ↑ cellular metabolism · ↑ inflammation

Indications: joint stiffness, contractures, low back pain

Increases blood flow → brings O2 and WBCs → aids healing; promotes relaxation, ↓ stiffness/muscle tension

Moist heat (washcloth, gauze compress, soaks) or dry heat

Cold Therapy

Effects: vasoconstriction · ↓ capillary permeability · ↓ cellular metabolism · slowed bacterial growth · ↓ inflammation/edema · local anesthetic effect

Indications: fractures, post-injury swelling, bleeding, sprains, strains

Heat/Cold Nursing Interventions

  • PCP prescription required: body site, type, frequency, duration
  • Assess site and patient's perception of temp changes
  • V/S pre & post treatment; inspect skin every 15 min
  • Receptors adapt to temp ~15 min — warn patient NOT to change temp (can cause tissue injury)
  • Monitor carefully: extremes of age, sensory impairment, highly vascular areas (fingers, hand, face, perineum), large body area, injured skin/wounds
Hyperbaric Treatment & Wound Culture

Hyperbaric Oxygen

Body exposed to 100% O2 at greater than normal pressure

Speeds healing by stimulating new blood vessel growth

Used for: diabetic wounds, burns

Wound Culture & Diagnostics

Obtain AFTER wound is irrigated/cleaned

BEST obtained PRIOR to antibiotic administration

Other dx: needle aspiration, tissue biopsy

Labs: albumin (protein), WBC (infection), coagulation (bleeding), glucose (diabetes)

Routes of Administration
Oral

Swallowed; absorbed in stomach or small intestines

Forms: pill, capsule, tablet, syrup, suspension, elixir, extract, tincture, solution, powder in liquid

Enteral

Given directly into stomach or intestines via tube (NG, G-tube); liquids only

Must check if pills can be crushed or capsules opened

Use for swallowing impairment (alternative to parenteral)

Disadvantages: not all tabs/caps can be crushed (can clog NG); NG = aspiration risk

Transmucosal

Sublingual: under tongue; do NOT swallow; allow to dissolve & absorb; example = NITROGLYCERIN

Buccal: held against mucous membrane in cheek; liquid, lozenges, spray, drops; examples = liquid Roxinol (end-of-life), Methyltestosterone

Can also be sprayed into nasal/pharyngeal mucosa

Topical

Skin: creams, lotions, ointment, transdermal patch, foam/spray, paste

Instillations (into a body cavity): eye, ear, nose, rectum, vagina, bladder — drops, spray, suppository, liquid

Inhalation: drug broken into fine particles via nebulizer; aerosols, inhalers, anesthesia gases

Parenteral — Into Body WITHOUT Digestive System

IV (Intravenous): directly in vein; rapid effect & absorption; one stick for multiple doses; risk of infection, vein problems; antibiotics, IV fluids, antineoplastics

IM (Intramuscular): into muscle mass; larger volume; risk of nerve damage; can be irritating; CAN'T use where tissue is damaged; vaccines, antibiotics, opioids, antiemetics — example Prochlorperazine

SQ (Subcutaneous): into subQ tissue; faster than oral; absorption SLOW; small amounts only; INSULIN, HEPARIN, LOVENOX; epinephrine

ID (Intradermal): under skin into dermis; very small amounts; slowly absorbed; PPD (TB test), allergy testing, lidocaine for local anesthesia

Parenteral — Other Routes

Intraspinal: into spinal canal

Intrathecal: into subarachnoid space around spinal cord

Epidural: between vertebral spines into extradural space; regional anesthesia & pain control

Rectal: in rectum — example Bisacodyl

Pharmacokinetics — "ADME"

Branch of pharmacology concerned with the movement of drugs within the body. 4 processes determine intensity & duration of drug actions.


A — Absorption

Movement of drug from route of administration INTO bloodstream; when drug becomes available systemically to do its intended action

Absorption depends on:

  • Route: IV faster than oral
  • Drug solubility: water-soluble = faster in GI; lipid-soluble = penetrate cell membranes; enteric-coated = dissolves in intestines (delays); time-release = slow, small amounts over hours
  • Blood flow to area: poor blood supply = poor absorption
  • Age: older adults have ↓ stomach acid, ↓ intestinal motility, ↓ muscle mass & subQ tissue
  • Patient condition
D — Distribution

How drug is delivered/moved in the bloodstream/circulatory system to the desired site

M — Metabolism

Once at site, drug breaks down & prepares for excretion

PRIMARY SITE = LIVER

E — Excretion

Removal/elimination of drug from the body

PRIMARY SITE = KIDNEYS

Pharmacodynamics

Definition: Study of how meds obtain effects in the body & how the body interacts/responds — the drug's biochemical & physiological effects on the body. (What the drug does to your body.)

Affected by: disorder/disease · aging process · other drugs


Primary Effects

Also called therapeutic effect — PREDICTED, DESIRED, INTENDED

Secondary Effects — All Unintended

ALL effects different from what was intended

Types of Secondary Effects
TypeDefinitionKey Points
Side EffectsUnintended but PREDICTABLE physiological effects; usually well toleratedCan CONTINUE drug; most common: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, dizziness, drowsiness, dry mouth, constipation; not life-threatening
Adverse ReactionsUnintended & HARMFUL; usually unpredicted; more severe than side effectsMUST STOP drug; severe ones are life-threatening — require intervention to prevent permanent impairment/death
Toxic ReactionsDAMAGE organs & tissue; can cause deathFrom overdosing (e.g., resp depression from excess morphine, hypoglycemia from too much insulin), accumulation in tissues, abnormal sensitivity
Allergic ReactionsIMMUNE SYSTEM reaction; common with antibiotics, biological agents, diagnostic agentsMinor → severe: hives (urticaria), itching (pruritus), edema, rhinitis → ANAPHYLAXIS: laryngeal edema, bronchoconstriction, hypotension
Idiosyncratic ReactionsUNEXPECTED, abnormal, peculiar responseCan be extreme sensitivity, lack of response, or PARADOXICAL (opposite of expected) — e.g., Benadryl causing hyperactivity in a child

Drug Interactions

Agonist — 1+1=2

Desired combined effects; drugs work together as intended

Antagonistic — 1+1=1

One drug LIMITS or BLOCKS effects of another; combined effect LESS than either alone

Synergistic — 1+1=3

Additional/additive effects; both together GREATER than individual effects

Potentiating — A+B=AAAAAA

One drug significantly enhances the other to the point of TOXICITY

Incompatibilities — 1+1=0

Mixed drugs cause chemical deterioration

  • Recognize: clear IV gets cloudy, particles in solution
  • Do NOT administer — consult compatibility charts BEFORE mixing
  • After mixing, double-check for changes
  • Administering incompatible meds = MEDICATION ERROR
Mobility Lecture 4

☑ Objectives

Physiology of Movement

Mobility depends on successful interaction among the Skeleton, Muscles, and Nervous System.


Skeletal System

  • Bones: 206; hard outer shell with spongy interior
  • Joints: where 2 bones articulate (hip, knee)
  • Cartilage: connective tissue; cushion around joints
  • Ligaments: fibrous tissue that connect movable joints (bone to bone)
  • Tendons: fibrous connective tissue that connect muscle to bone

Muscles

  • ~Half of body weight
  • Contract → cause movement
  • 3 types: skeletal (moves bones/joints), smooth, cardiac

Motor Nervous System

  • Controls movement of musculoskeletal system
  • Motor nerves: autonomic OR somatic
  • Somatic NS: controls voluntary skeletal muscles
Body Mechanics

Definition: The way we move our bodies; allows movement without injury if done properly. 4 components: body alignment, balance, coordination, joint mobility.

ANA (2013): Use mechanical equipment (transfer board, lift) OR get assistance whenever possible. Do not rely on body mechanics alone.


Avoiding Injury — Key Principles

  • Use proper alignment = neutral spine posture
  • Wide base of support (feet spread apart) = balance
  • Avoid bending and twisting; face the object
  • Squat to lift (lowers center of gravity; position close to object)
  • Keep objects close when lifting; raise bed to waist height
  • Push, slide, or pull rather than lift whenever possible
  • Maintain good grip before moving
  • Keep elbows bent when carrying
  • Smooth & even movements (avoid jerky) = COORDINATION
  • Use mechanical equipment or get assistance
  • Teach patients/families too

Assess Patient Before Transfer

  • Patient's ability to assist in transfer
  • Patient's strength & ability to bear weight
  • Height & weight factor into safe transfer
Range of Motion (ROM)

Goal: Exercise & keep body in best possible physical condition when bed rest or immobility is present. Improve joint mobility, increase circulation, help maintain function, prevent contractures.

ROM = the maximum movement possible at a joint.


AROM — Active Range of Motion

Done BY PATIENT WITHOUT ASSISTANCE

Patient independently moves joints through flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, circular motions

Often in rehab as pt recovering from illness, injury, surgery

Must teach patient what to do and how often

PROM — Passive Range of Motion

Done BY ANOTHER PERSON FOR PATIENT because they CANNOT do it themselves

Intervention to prevent complications of disuse & promote joint mobility

Prevents contractures, muscle atrophy

Factors Affecting Activity & Mobility

Patient Factors

  1. Developmental Stage (age): older adults least active; 30 min/day recommended; incorporate resistance & balance training to prevent falls
  2. Cognitive status: can pt participate?
  3. Nutrition: obesity & chronic disease d/t inadequate protein stores (albumin)
  4. Lifestyle: sedentary

External Factors

  1. Stress: releases cortisol/adrenaline → muscle tension, fatigue, anxiety, brain damage, balance deficits
  2. Environmental factors
  3. Diseases & abnormalities
  4. Respiratory disorders, circulatory disorders, fatigue, bed rest
Complications of Immobility by System
Musculoskeletal — First Systems Affected

Complications:

  • Balance problems; loss of endurance
  • Decreased muscle mass / ATROPHY — 10% muscle strength loss per week
  • Wasting of gastrocnemius, soleus, leg muscles (control hip/knee/ankle)
  • Joints become stiff; decreased stability
  • Joint contractures — strongest muscles/flexors pull joints → ankylosis
  • Disuse osteoporosis — bones that don't bear weight release calcium
  • Hypercalcemia

Interventions:

  • Assist pt to standing & ambulation as tolerated
  • Prevent orthostatic hypotension
  • Use positioning devices to maintain body alignment
  • AROM, PROM
  • Assistive devices: walker, crutches, cane, bathing/toileting devices
Respiratory

Complications:

  • ↓ lung expansion & generalized respiratory muscle weakness
  • Stasis & pooling of secretions
  • Risk for atelectasis (collapse of air sacs) or pneumonia
  • Pooled secretions block alveoli → ↓ gas exchange

Why: Chest movement restricted by bed; abdominal organs press on diaphragm; chest muscles weaken; secretions pool in bronchi → can't be expelled → atelectasis & pneumonia.

Interventions:

  • Orthopneic position
  • T&P q2h
  • Cough & deep breathe
  • Incentive spirometer
  • Increase fluids to liquefy secretions (as tolerated)
Cardiovascular

Complications:

  • Edema; venous stasis
  • Thrombus formation (DVT)
  • Orthostatic hypotension; blood pooling
  • ↑ heart rate
  • ↓ Cardiac reserve (heart less able to respond to body's demands)

Why: Muscle contraction needed to pump blood back to heart. Immobility → blood stagnant, pools in leg veins → edema, stasis → thrombus.

Interventions:

  • DVT prophylaxis
  • ROM to help prevent edema
  • Monitor heart rate
Metabolic

Complications:

  • ↓ Serum albumin = edema (low protein → water leaves vessels into tissues)
  • ↓ Protein stores = muscle loss/atrophy
  • Calcium leaves bones = hypercalcemia, osteoporosis, renal calculi, calcium deposits in joints
  • ↓ BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate)
  • Protein & glycogen synthesis ↓; fat stores ↑

Interventions:

  • See GI interventions
  • Osteoporosis: weight-bearing exercise (especially older women) to improve bone density & reduce bone loss
Gastrointestinal

Complications:

  • Anorexia: appetite diminishes; digestion slows from ↓ metabolic rate → ↓ calorie intake; muscle broken down as fuel
  • Constipation: slows peristalsis → constipation, gas, difficulty evacuating
  • PARALYTIC ILEUS — cessation of peristalsis — can occur

Interventions:

  • Small frequent meals; high calorie, high protein diet
  • Monitor weight
  • ↑ fluids as tolerated; ↑ fiber in diet
  • Monitor BMs (COCAF)
  • Stool softeners as needed
Genitourinary

Complications:

  • Renal calculi; decreased urinary output
  • Urinary stasis; UTIs
  • Urinary retention (↓ muscle tone → ↓ bladder tone)

Why: Supine position inhibits drainage of urine from kidney/bladder → stagnant urine = ideal for infection. ↑ Calcium → stone formation. Many pts have difficulty voiding in bedpan/urinal.

Interventions:

  • Monitor I&O
  • Encourage & increase fluids per patient condition
  • Sit upright or get OOB to urinate
Integumentary

Complications:

  • Pressure injuries — pressure over bony prominences compresses capillaries → ↓ circulation → tissue hypoxia → tissue damage

Interventions:

  • T&P q2h
  • Pressure-reducing devices; trapeze; turn sheets; friction-reducing devices
  • If incontinent: keep area clean & dry
Psychosocial

Complications:

  • Depression; behavioral changes; sleep disturbances; coping problems
  • Isolation; sensory deprivation; increased dependence
  • Affect: moodiness, anxiety, hostility, apathy, poor body concept
  • Cognition: ↓ concentration, recall, problem-solving
  • Self-care: reduced ability to perform

Interventions:

  • Provide socialization; place in room with others
  • Social worker consult
  • Activities
Mobility Assessment

Assess on Admission & Each Shift

  • Level of comfort; LOC
  • Restrictions in movement/position
  • Physical size of patient; presence of equipment
  • Side effects of medication; vital signs (orthostatic hypotension)
  • Risk factors for falls; underlying health conditions
  • Use of assistive devices
  • Body alignment, posture; joint function & ROM
  • Muscle strength (push/pull against your hands)
  • Compare muscle mass right & left
  • Activity tolerance (vital signs)

Patients on Bed Rest

Regularly monitor ALL body systems for complications of immobility — musculoskeletal, respiratory, cardiovascular, metabolic, GI, GU, integumentary, psychosocial.

Factors Contributing to Surgical Risk

General Risk Factors

  • Age: very young & very old are at greatest risk
  • Type of wound: pre-existing or post-op
  • Pre-existing conditions: active infections, pneumonia, cardiac, COPD, coagulation, diabetes, liver, kidney, neurological, spinal cord injury, seizure
  • Mental status: confused/mental illness may not comprehend pre-op instructions or give informed consent; antipsychotics may interact with anesthetics
  • Personal habits: smoking (pulmonary), alcohol (liver/bleeding), drug interactions
  • Allergies: antibiotics, analgesics, latex, anesthesia, shellfish

Medication Risks

  • Anticoagulants → ↑ bleeding
  • Antihypertensives → ↑ hypotension during surgery; may interact with anesthesia → bradycardia, impaired circulation
  • Aspirin → ↑ bleeding
  • Corticosteroids → delay wound healing, ↑ infection risk
  • Opioids → ↑ respiratory depression
  • Antibiotics → may potentiate anesthetic agents & mask new infections
  • Antidysrhythmics → may impair cardiac function during anesthesia
  • Diuretics → alter fluid & electrolyte balance (especially potassium)
  • NSAIDs → inhibit platelet aggregation, ↑ bleeding
  • Tranquilizers → ↑ respiratory depression
Older Adult Surgical Risk Factors

Cardiovascular & Respiratory

  • Coronary artery disease; ↑ risk hypotension, falls
  • DELIRIUM — most common surgical complication in older adults
  • ↓ Chest wall compliance, ↓ FVC, ↓ diaphragm strength → risk for pneumonia, atelectasis
  • ↓ Cardiac output; ↑ peripheral vascular resistance

Skin, Musculoskeletal & CNS

  • Dry, fragile skin, ↓ turgor → risk for pressure ulcer, impaired skin integrity
  • ↓ Bone & muscle mass → risk for falls, impaired mobility
  • CNS comorbidities → risk dementia, acute confusion

GI & GU

  • ↓ GI motility → risk ileus, aspiration from vomiting
  • ↓ GU function → risk medication side effects, renal complications, UTI
  • ↓ Kidney function → slower drug excretion → toxicity risk

Informed Consent / Surgical Consent

What Consent Includes

  • Type of surgery
  • Risks & benefits
  • Statement that patient has the right to refuse

Purpose

  • Protects patient from unwanted or misunderstood surgery
  • Protects healthcare agency & workers from later claims of non-consent

Roles

SURGEON: explains procedure, risks & benefits; obtains patient signature; determines patient's competence to make an informed decision

NURSE: verifies consent has been signed & witnessed; can witness the signing; verifies it is in the medical record

Postoperative Phase 1 — PACU (Post Anesthesia Care Unit)
PHASE ONE = patient at HIGHEST risk for respiratory & cardiac compromise. Anesthesiologist & circulating nurse bring pt to PACU and give report. PACU nurse takes over: assessment q5–15 min. If unconscious: position on side.

PACU Priority Assessment — ABC + Nausea/Pain

  1. Airway: if unconscious, turn on side; patency, artificial airway, ventilator settings; assess chest rising and falling
  2. Breathing: respiratory rate, lung sounds, skin color; pulse oximetry (>90%)
  3. Circulation: BP, pulse rate; drainage on dressing; peripheral pulses, swelling, hematoma
  4. Nausea & Pain: vomiting can obstruct airway (pt may be asleep/drowsy/intubated)

Evidence of Recovery from Anesthesia

  • Airway: pt maintains patent airway independently; can deep-breathe, cough, expectorate
  • LOC: conscious and easily reoriented; may drift to sleep but easily reorient
  • Vital signs: stable, within acceptable range
  • Mobility & sensation: can move all extremities they could pre-op; regains movement once spinal/epidural wears off
  • Fluid balance (I&O): urinating ≥30 mL/hr, in relative balance
  • Dressings & drains: dry & intact OR wound drainage appropriate for procedure

  • Carbonated drinks

Full Liquids

All clear liquids PLUS:

  • Vegetable juices

Assess BEFORE Starting Any Diet

6 Things to Check

  1. HCP orders
  2. LOC — would you give an unconscious person a drink? NO
  3. Can they swallow? If can't swallow saliva → can't swallow food/liquid
  4. Gag reflex? Without gag reflex → choke and aspirate
  5. GI tract functioning? Bowel sounds, belching/burping, passing flatus
  6. Nauseous or vomiting?

Why This Matters

Aspiration pneumonia results from inhaling gastric contents — caused by absent gag reflex secondary to anesthesia. This is why we assess ALL these factors before any oral intake.

3 Surgical Drains — Nurse Responsibilities

Surgically-placed drains let fluids/exudate exit wounds → ↓ pressure → prevents infection, dehiscence, evisceration.

Maintain ALL drains BELOW the incision site. Report any significant change in amount or character of drainage to the provider. If no drainage, may be occluded — remove kinks.

1. Penrose Drain

Flat flexible tube — LATEX (know allergies pre-op)

Positioned in wound bed — NOT sutured

Measure exposed parts to track from assessment to assessment

May be advanced if ordered

2. Jackson-Pratt (JP)

Removes fluid via SUCTION — must COMPRESS the device for suction

Measure at intervals ordered or per shift

EMPTY when ½ FULL — never wait until completely full (no suction)

Check: full? compressed? tubing kinked? change in drainage or pain?

3. Hemovac

Removes fluid via SUCTION — must compress device for suction

Measure at intervals ordered or per shift

Check: full? compressed? tubing kinked? change in drainage or pain?

Any significant change → report to provider

Dehiscence vs. Evisceration
Dehiscence — Wound Layer Separation

Definition: Separation of ONE OR MORE layers of wound (NOT total)

Causes: poor nutrition, obesity, strain on suture line, inadequate closure, infection

Interventions:

  • Cover with DRY STERILE DRESSING
  • Maintain BEDREST; HOB at 20° & knees flexed
  • Apply BINDER to prevent evisceration
  • Notify provider; continue to monitor
Evisceration — SURGICAL EMERGENCY

Definition: TOTAL separation of wound layers with internal viscera protruding through

Interventions:

  • IMMEDIATELY cover with STERILE TOWELS soaked with STERILE SALINE (prevents organs from drying & contamination)
  • Bedrest with KNEES BENT (prevent strain on incision)
  • HOB 20 degrees
  • DO NOT apply a binder
  • Notify surgeon and PREP FOR SURGERY
Post-Op Complications
Hemorrhage — Risk Greatest First 24–48 Hours

EXTERNAL (visible):

  • Dressing saturated with bright red blood
  • Increased blood in drains
  • Dependent drainage — ALWAYS check under pt for pooled drainage if dressing has blood

INTERNAL (not easily detected):

  • Increased pain; edema/swelling around incision; hematoma; ecchymosis

Vital signs: TACHYCARDIA + FALLING BP (HYPOTENSION)

Interventions:

  • Assess pt post-op; monitor vital signs
  • Assess dressing, wound drainage, skin around incision, drains
  • Patient who is unresponsive, nonverbal, or confused can't tell you what they feel — you must assess
Infection

S/S: swelling, redness, heat, pain, fever >100.4°F, ↑ HR, foul/purulent drainage, wound not healing

Contaminated/traumatic wound: symptoms in 2–3 days

Clean surgical wound: usually not until the 4th or 5th post-op day

Interventions:

  • Monitor for s/s; monitor V/S
  • STERILE technique for dressing changes
  • Hand hygiene; culture if prescribed
  • Labs (WBC will be elevated)
Thrombus — Thrombophlebitis → Pulmonary Embolism

Thrombophlebitis (blood clot + vein inflammation):

  • Caused by stasis of blood
  • S/S: leg red, hot to touch, edematous, aching, cramping
  • Treatment: immobilize limb; may have HEPARIN

Pulmonary Embolism (clot occludes blood flow to lungs):

  • Usually a clot from lower extremities that breaks loose and migrates
  • S/S: SUDDEN onset dyspnea, SOB, chest pain, hypotension, tachycardia, ↓ SaO2, cyanosis
  • Prevention = prevent DVT (leg exercises, stockings, SCDs, early ambulation)
Respiratory Complications

Pneumonia: Inflammation of alveoli d/t infection; caused by HYPOVENTILATION secondary to anesthesia & opioids; small airways fill with solid material instead of air

S/S: productive cough (blood-tinged or purulent), fever/chills, ↑ WBC, ↓ or absent lung sounds, low SaO2, dyspnea, tachycardia, tachypnea, malaise, pleural pain

Aspiration Pneumonia: Airway inflammation from inhaling gastric secretions (HCl) d/t absent gag reflex secondary to anesthesia

S/S: cough, fever, ↑ WBC, ↓ breath sounds, ↓ SaO2, tachypnea, dyspnea, blood-tinged sputum

Prevention: NPO ≥8 hrs pre-op; continue NPO until motility returns; side-lying if sedated; antiemetics; prevent N&V

Pain — Classification (3 Ways)
By Origin
  • Cutaneous/superficial: skin, subQ — burn, abrasion, IV infiltration
  • Deep somatic: ligaments, tendons, nerve, bone, blood vessels — fracture, torn ACL, sprain
  • Visceral: deep internal pain receptors (organs) — abdominal cramps, labor, GI infection
  • Radiating/referred: starts in one area, extends to another — sciatica back→leg; GERD epigastric→chest
  • Phantom: pain/burning/itching in a MISSING extremity
  • Psychogenic: no physical cause identified; perceived as severe as physical pain
By Cause

Nociceptive: pain receptors respond to potentially damaging stimuli; injury to body tissue — trauma, surgery, inflammation

Neuropathic: complex, usually chronic; injury to nerves; repeated pain signals in absence of stimuli; burning, numbness, pins/needles — DM, stroke, tumor, shingles, spinal

By Duration

Acute: rapid onset, short duration (up to 6 months); r/t injury & surgery

Chronic: 3–6 months or longer; interferes with daily living; progressive disorders or neuropathic

Intractable: chronic & highly resistant to relief; needs multiple pain relief methods

Factors That Influence Pain

Emotions

Fear (death, intolerable suffering, addiction, judgment); confusion & helplessness; anger, guilt, loneliness; anxiety & depression

Past Experience

Anxiety & fear of experiencing pain again; increased sensitivity to pain

Developmental Stage

Older adults often have daily pain interfering with ADLs; cognitively impaired can't properly report pain

Sociocultural Factors

Every patient reacts differently; pain beliefs play a role (some smile, deny, yell, cry); be mindful of your own beliefs — don't judge others' response

Cognitive Impairment

May fail to interpret sensations; can't communicate pain; unable to recall pain — may be climbing OOB, yelling, irritable, refusing to move

Numeric Rating Scale (NRS)Line numbered 0–10; 0=no pain, 10=worst possible; mild 1–3, moderate 4–6, severe 7–10Adults who can count to 10; must explain endpoints; 0–5 scale may help cognitively impaired
Visual Analog Scale (VAS)10-cm horizontal line; "No pain" left end, "Worst pain imaginable" right; pt points to locationSimple and quick; may be too abstract for some pts
Wong-Baker FACESSimple illustrated faces from happy (0) to crying (5 = "hurts as much as you can imagine")Children >3 years; adults with communication or cognitive impairment; no reading or number skill needed
FLACC ScaleBehavioral scale: Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability — each scored 0, 1, or 2Children 2 months–7 years; nonverbal/pre-verbal patients; ventilated, unresponsive, or confused individuals
Nonverbal Indicators of Pain

Physiological Responses (Acute / Sympathetic)

  • ↑ Systolic BP; ↑ HR & force of contraction
  • ↑ RR, shallow breathing; dilated pupils
  • Rapid speech; impaired GI motility
  • Prolonged pain → ↓ BP & pulse, withdrawal, constricted pupils

Behavioral Responses

  • Moaning / groaning
  • Facial grimacing / frowning
  • Crying; agitation / fidgeting
  • Withdrawal from painful stimuli
  • Guarding or rubbing the painful area

Cognitively Impaired — Less Obvious

  • Facial expressions (sad/frightened, rapid eye blinking)
  • Vocalizations (noisy breathing, profanity, verbally abusive)
  • Changes in activity (fidgeting, pacing, rocking, disruptive)
  • Changes in routines (refusing food, difficulty sleeping)
  • Mental status changes (increased confusion)
  • Physiological cues (↑ BP, RR, pulse)
Absence of these cues does NOT automatically mean pain is absent.

Opioid Analgesics

Side Effects

  • Respiratory depression
  • Sedation; nausea; vomiting
  • Constipation; urinary retention
  • Facial flushing; itching

Nursing Responsibilities

  • Baseline vital signs before administration
  • Pain assessment BEFORE & AFTER
  • Stool softeners, fluids, dietary fiber
  • Reversal agent: Naloxone (Narcan)
OPIOIDS CAN CAUSE RESPIRATORY DEPRESSION. ALWAYS assess respiratory status BEFORE & AFTER administering opioids. If RR drops to <10 breaths/min: STOP opioids → administer NALOXONE (NARCAN). Monitor: RR & O2 sat (airway/breathing), V/S & pulse (circulation), sedation level — are they easily aroused?
Pharmacological Caveats

Key Principles

  • Analgesics work best given BEFORE pain becomes severe
  • Administer at REGULAR times (ATC = around the clock), not PRN
  • NO ceiling on opioid dosing
  • Addiction RARE in short-term opioid use for pain
  • After giving pain med, REASSESS at onset/peak; if still unacceptable → contact PCP
  • Pt won't be able to participate in surgical recovery if in pain

PRN Medications

ATC = Around The Clock

PRN = as needed

Must check when last given — do NOT administer before due

Example: Morphine q4h PRN — assess pain, review MD order, check when pt last received it

PCA — Patient Controlled Analgesia
Modes & Nursing Responsibilities

Mode 1: patient pushes button for dose

Mode 2: patient pushes button for dose PLUS machine administers constant basal dose

Responsibilities:

  • Verify order, allergies & pump settings — 2 RNs
  • Assess V/S, pain level, cognition
  • Patient must be on CONTINUOUS PULSE OXIMETRY — does NOT omit need for reassessments
  • NEVER push the pump button for the pt; never allow others to push it
  • Educate pt: use BEFORE activities (ambulating, incentive spirometer); call before getting OOB; rise slowly
Pros & Cons of PCA

Pros:

  • Patient-controlled; consistent pain management
  • Prevents waiting for nurse
  • Can pre-medicate before activity

Cons:

  • Risk of respiratory depression — requires continuous pulse ox
  • Requires alert pt who can press the button
  • Risk if family or staff push the button for the pt
